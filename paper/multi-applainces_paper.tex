\begin{abstract}
Appliance-level load visibility supports residential demand-side management, but aggregate meters do not directly reveal individual consumption or operating states. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) estimates these quantities without dedicated appliance sensors. Multi-appliance disaggregation shares computation across loads, yet sparse activations under limited background conditions and unequal power--state loss scales complicate joint learning. This paper proposes the Multi-Appliance Relational Temporal Network for NILM (MART-NILM), a compact joint estimator. A shared temporal convolutional encoder, appliance-specific decoders, and relational attention jointly estimate five appliance powers and states. Focal-event background-stratified mixing exposes active appliance traces to varied residual loads, while bounded appliance-wise balancing adjusts each classification objective relative to its corresponding regression loss. Both strategies operate only during training. Experiments on two public residential datasets use disjoint training, validation, and test houses and preserve recorded aggregates. Across three seeds, MART-NILM achieves appliance-average mean absolute errors of 7.5 and 10.8 watts and macro-averaged F1 scores of 0.88 and 0.72 on the respective test houses, outperforming three reproduced baselines on both measures. With 1.373 million parameters and lower training time under the evaluated configurations, the method offers a favorable estimation--cost trade-off for residential load monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Residential energy management requires information about both the magnitude and operating state of individual loads. Aggregate metering provides household demand, but not this appliance-level visibility. Dedicated submetering can supply detailed feedback~\cite{delmas2013information}, at the cost of installing and maintaining additional sensors. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) estimates appliance consumption from aggregate measurements and offers a less instrument-intensive basis for residential load analysis~\cite{zoha2012nonintrusive,gopinath2020energy}. To support residential load observability, a NILM estimator must identify appliance activity as well as reconstruct appliance power: low average power error can conceal missed operating periods, particularly for appliances that remain off for most of the record.

Classical NILM identifies appliance signatures and infers appliance states with finite-state or factorial hidden Markov models~\cite{hart1992nilm,kolter2012fhmm}. Deep approaches instead learn representations directly from aggregate windows~\cite{kelly2015neural}, reducing the need to specify signatures and state-transition models manually. Regardless of the estimator, a recorded aggregate contains demand outside the monitored target set. For $N$ target appliances, the measured power is
\begin{equation}
x_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_{t,i} + b_t + \epsilon_t,
\label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $P_{t,i}$ is target power, $b_t$ is unmonitored household demand, and $\epsilon_t$ represents measurement and timing discrepancies. This residual background can exceed a low-power target and varies between households, so a familiar appliance event can occur in an unfamiliar aggregate context. House-held-out evaluation on recorded aggregates is therefore needed; results from different sensing and partitioning protocols are not directly interchangeable~\cite{dasilva2026nilm}.

Within this setting, two common deep NILM modelling paradigms are single-appliance and multi-appliance estimation. Single-appliance networks predict one target per model and have advanced disaggregation accuracy~\cite{wang2022transformer,sun2023diffnilm,petralia2025nilmformer}, but repeating a model for every load repeats aggregate feature extraction and increases storage requirements. Recent deep NILM work has therefore increasingly studied multi-appliance estimation, in which one model jointly estimates a fixed set of $N$ target appliances from the same aggregate window~\cite{faustine2020unet,alami2023convnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget,sun2025transfer}. NILM research commonly considers two complementary tasks: power estimation and ON--OFF state detection. For appliance $i$ at time $t$, these tasks estimate a power trace $\hat y_{t,i}$ and an ON-state probability $\hat s_{t,i}$, respectively. Power estimation is a regression task, whereas state detection is multi-label classification in the multi-appliance setting because several appliances may be ON at the same time. For an aggregate window $\mathbf{x}$ of length $T$, the joint estimator is
\begin{equation}
\left(\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\hat{\mathbf{S}}\right)=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N},\quad
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in[0,1]^{T\times N},
\label{eq:joint-estimation}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ collect the estimated power traces and ON-state probabilities, respectively.

Joint multi-appliance and multi-label estimation is more challenging than running $N$ independent predictors. Concurrent activations provide useful cross-appliance context, but their superposed transitions can mask a weak target beneath other loads or the residual background~\cite{faustine2020unet,alami2023convnilm,xiong2024matnilm}. A joint architecture must distinguish appliance-specific evidence within the shared aggregate and support coherent power--state outputs. Explicit cross-appliance interaction offers one way to incorporate relational context. Shared feature extraction can also reduce repeated computation across appliances.

Training raises two further difficulties. First, sparse events in a finite record occur under only a subset of residual-background conditions. A model can then use residual-background correlations as a shortcut instead of the appliance signature; this can fail when those correlations change across households~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. Second, appliance power ranges and on-state prevalences differ substantially. Existing joint objectives use a shared regression--classification balance, either as a fixed coefficient or a global loss-ratio weight~\cite{sun2025transfer,liu2022samnet}. Such a shared balance cannot represent appliance-specific scales; when derived from joint losses, a large power error for one appliance also changes the relative state weight of the others. These observations motivate control over both residual-background conditions and the power--state balance of each appliance.

To address these issues, we propose the Multi-Appliance Relational Temporal Network for NILM (MART-NILM), a relational temporal model for joint power and ON--OFF state estimation under residual-background variation.

The main contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A compact multi-appliance architecture, MART-NILM, is designed for
    joint power and state estimation. It extracts a shared temporal
    representation once and combines appliance-specific decoding with
    cross-appliance relational context to produce five paired power--state
    outputs in a single forward pass.

    \item Two training-only strategies address sparse events,
    residual-background variation, and appliance-wise power--state imbalance.
    Focal-event background-stratified mixing exposes activations to a controlled
    range of recorded residual-background conditions, while bounded
    appliance-wise balancing independently aligns the power and state
    objectives of each appliance. Neither strategy adds inference-time
    computation.

    \item A house-held-out evaluation uses recorded aggregates from UK-DALE
    and REFIT. Three-seed
    baseline comparisons, architecture and training ablations, and
    background-conditioned analyses quantify power estimation, state
    detection, computational cost, and remaining failure modes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

Multi-appliance and multi-label NILM estimates power and predicts an ON--OFF label for each appliance in a fixed set from a single aggregate measurement. This setting has a nested multi-task structure: the inner level couples power regression and state classification for each appliance, whereas the outer level determines how representations are shared across appliances that may be active concurrently. Existing NILM studies recognize these two levels through formulations described as ``multi-task-multi-appliance''~\cite{faustine2020unet} and ``multi-appliance-task''~\cite{xiong2024matnilm,sun2025transfer}.

Most generic multi-task learning (MTL) formulations regard tasks as a single peer set and learn which parameters, experts, or intermediate representations should be shared across them~\cite{vandenhende2022survey,ma2018mmoe}. In multi-appliance and multi-label NILM, however, task relations are structured rather than flat. The model must couple heterogeneous power-regression and state-classification targets within each appliance while separating and relating appliances that may be active concurrently in the same aggregate. A suitable sharing design must therefore preserve appliance-specific signatures without discarding useful cross-appliance context.

Relevant NILM studies, including multi-appliance and multi-label formulations, use convolutional neural networks (CNNs)~\cite{faustine2020unet,alami2023convnilm}, recurrent neural networks (RNNs)~\cite{murray2019transferability}, temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) with attention~\cite{xiong2024matnilm,dash2024jestie}, and Transformers~\cite{sun2025transfer}. Backbone choice controls temporal feature extraction; the remaining architectural question is how those features are organized for joint prediction. In joint multi-appliance and multi-label estimation, this organization entails two linked design decisions: \emph{appliance-head design}, which governs within-appliance power--state output coupling, and \emph{cross-appliance body design}, which governs where appliance representations separate, remain shared, or exchange information before reaching the appliance heads. Figures~\ref{fig:appliance-head} and~\ref{fig:model-body-design} summarize these two levels, respectively.

At the appliance-head level, early joint NILM designs use separate state and power branches, with the predicted state concatenated with power-branch features before the final regression layers. The CNN and RNN models of Murray \emph{et al.}~\cite{murray2019transferability}, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(a), use this state-informed concatenation. It allows the regressor to use state information while retaining direct power features, but does not explicitly enforce consistency between OFF predictions and power estimates.

An explicit connection is introduced by the subtask-gated network (SGN)~\cite{shin2019sgn}, which multiplies the regression output by a separately supervised on-state probability, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(b). This gate suppresses power during predicted inactivity, although a missed activation also attenuates an otherwise valid power estimate. The basic formulation suppresses standby consumption, motivating a standby-power variant in the original study~\cite{shin2019sgn}.

To accommodate different event durations and temporal contexts, the scale- and context-aware network (SCANet)~\cite{chen2021sca} extends SGN with parallel dilated branches and self-attention. Figure~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(c) summarizes this design: classification features gate the corresponding regression features at each scale, followed by separate feature fusion and contextual processing, while the final output retains state gating. This richer coupling supports feature selection at multiple temporal scales, at the cost of additional branches and attention computation, and retains sensitivity to state-derived gates. Thus, Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(c) extends the gating design in Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(b), rather than representing an unrelated alternative. These appliance-head mechanisms can be combined with different ways of sharing information across appliances.

The resulting design question is where to share information across appliances. Multi-task architectures commonly share an encoder and retain task-specific outputs, or continue exchanging information during decoding~\cite{vandenhende2022survey}. MATNilm explicitly branches by appliance and then by regression and classification task~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}; Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(a) isolates this hierarchical branch pattern. Its full decoder also includes temporal and appliance-wise attention. Shared encoding reduces repeated feature extraction, while private branches retain capacity for different appliance signatures.

A fully shared body with task-specific output heads is another joint-estimation design. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(b), it retains one representation for all appliances, then uses separate heads to predict the vector of appliance powers and the vector of independent appliance-state labels. UNet-NILM is one example of this pattern~\cite{faustine2020unet}. This approach shares all body features, rather than maintaining appliance-specific streams before prediction.

Feature interaction, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(c), provides a complementary mechanism. In dense prediction, attention-guided message passing transfers information from intermediate task predictions to final tasks~\cite{xu2018padnet}. In NILM, MATNilm already models dependencies across appliances and time~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}. MART-NILM applies this information-sharing principle directly to appliance-specific feature streams before their power and state heads. The architectural trade-off is therefore how to retain appliance evidence and relational context at modest computational cost.

Architecture alone does not determine joint learning. Gradient normalization balances task updates directly~\cite{chen2018gradnorm}, whereas SAMNet uses a power--state loss ratio~\cite{liu2022samnet}. Background recomposition addresses a different issue: exposure to residual demand beyond the target set~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. Together, these studies motivate evaluating architectural sharing, appliance-wise power--state balance, and residual-background coverage.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Given aggregate power $x_t$, the task is to estimate the powers and ON--OFF states of $N$ target appliances. The recorded aggregate also includes unmonitored demand $b_t$ and measurement discrepancies $\epsilon_t$, leaving the unobserved residual
\begin{equation}
x_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{t,i}=b_{t}+\epsilon_{t}.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
For an aggregate window $\mathbf{x}$ of length $T$, the joint mapping in \eqref{eq:joint-estimation} produces paired outputs for every appliance and time step:
\begin{equation}
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta},t}(\mathbf{x})=
\underbrace{\bigl(\hat y_{t,1},\hat s_{t,1},\ldots,
\hat y_{t,N},\hat s_{t,N}\bigr)}_{N\text{ appliance power--state pairs}},
\quad t=1,\ldots,T.
\label{eq:paired-outputs}
\end{equation}
Here $\hat y_{t,i}$ estimates appliance power on the standardized scale, and $\hat s_{t,i}\in[0,1]$ is its ON-state probability. Power estimation is a regression task, whereas state detection is multi-label classification because several appliances can be ON simultaneously. Binary ON--OFF labels summarize activity without resolving internal operating modes.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/martnilm_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Model Architecture of MART-NILM.}
    \label{fig:martnilm-architecture}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:martnilm-architecture} presents MART-NILM, combining the private-stream interaction body in Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(c) with the state-gated heads in Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(b). The components are described below in processing order, using the same names as the architecture ablations in Table~\ref{tab:architecture-ablation}.

\textit{Multi-scale CNN:}
Parallel convolutional branches~\cite{szegedy2015inception} extract local features at complementary time scales from the standardized aggregate $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}=\mathcal{F}\!\left(
[\mathcal{B}_{3}(\bar{\mathbf{x}});\mathcal{B}_{5}(\bar{\mathbf{x}});
\mathcal{B}_{9}(\bar{\mathbf{x}})]\right)+\mathcal{S}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}).
\label{eq:multiscale-fusion}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{B}_{k}$ is a kernel-$k$ convolution followed by instance--batch normalization (IBN)~\cite{pan2018ibn} and a rectified linear unit (ReLU); $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation. The $1\times1$ fusion $\mathcal{F}$ combines the branches, while the projected shortcut $\mathcal{S}$ retains a direct input path. IBN applies instance normalization (IN) and batch normalization (BN) to separate channel halves.

\textit{Feature encoder:}
Two convolution--IBN--ReLU stages combine the local features into a shared representation $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times T}$. Feature extraction is performed once for all appliances.

\textit{Temporal encoder:}
Residual dilated TCN blocks~\cite{lea2017tcn} extend temporal context:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)}=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}+
\mathcal{T}_{2^{\ell}}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}),
\qquad \ell\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}.
\label{eq:temporal-block}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{T}_{d}$ comprises a dilation-$d$ convolution, BN, ReLU, and dropout. Increasing dilation expands context, while residual connections and symmetric padding retain the incoming features and temporal alignment. The output is $\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}^{(5)}$.

\textit{Appliance decoders:}
Each decoder applies a channel gate, inspired by channel recalibration~\cite{hu2018senet}, and a local residual transformation to obtain an appliance-specific representation $\mathbf{F}_i$. These private streams adapt the shared temporal features to individual load characteristics.

\textit{Relation attention:}
The relation module draws on the cross-task information-sharing principle of PAD-Net~\cite{xu2018padnet} to exchange context among appliance-specific feature streams. Scaled dot-product attention~\cite{vaswani2017attention} is evaluated across appliances at each time $t$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_t=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}_t\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt{d}}
\right),\qquad
\mathbf{M}_t=\mathbf{A}_t\mathbf{V}_t.
\label{eq:relation-attention}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{Q}_t$, $\mathbf{K}_t$, and $\mathbf{V}_t$ are learned projections of the stacked appliance representations, and $d$ is their projection width. The attended context is projected back to the feature dimension and added to each private stream through a gated residual connection.

\textit{Joint power and state prediction:}
For each appliance, two time-shared fully connected (FC) heads predict raw standardized power $\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)$ and a state logit $a_i(t)$. Following the state-gating principle of the subtask-gated network (SGN)~\cite{shin2019sgn}, the on-state probability $\hat s_i(t)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_i(t))$ softly gates the power prediction:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_i(t)=\hat s_i(t)\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)
+[1-\hat s_i(t)]y_i^{(0)},
\label{eq:soft-power-gate}
\end{equation}
where $y_i^{(0)}=-\mu_i/\sigma_i$ is the standardized zero-power anchor. Hence, a low on-state probability smoothly moves the estimate towards zero power. Predictions are converted back to watts using the appliance training mean $\mu_i$ and standard deviation $\sigma_i$, and negative values are clipped to zero. The gate couples the state and power predictions while retaining a separate waveform regressor.

\subsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss}

The model is trained jointly over all appliances, as in multi-task NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget}. Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}$ denote the power and state losses for appliance $i$, respectively. The joint objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}
+\lambda r_i\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}
\right),
\qquad \lambda=0.8.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here $r_i$ is an appliance-wise balancing factor defined below. Thus, every appliance contributes one power objective and one state objective to the single joint network. The constituent power and state losses, together with the appliance-wise balancing rule, are specified below.

\textit{Power loss:}
For a mini-batch of $B$ windows, each with $T$ samples, let $y_{b,t,i}$ and $\hat y_{b,t,i}$ denote the standardized target and predicted power for appliance $i$, and let $z_{b,t,i}\in\{0,1\}$ denote its on--off label. Define $e_{b,t,i}=\hat y_{b,t,i}-y_{b,t,i}$, $N_i^{\mathrm{on}}=\sum_{b,t}z_{b,t,i}$, and $N_i^{\mathrm{off}}=BT-N_i^{\mathrm{on}}$. The overall, on-state, and off-state mean squared errors are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{MSE}_i&=\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}e_{b,t,i}^{2},\nonumber\\
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{on}}&=
\tfrac{\sum_{b,t}z_{b,t,i}e_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{on}},1)},\nonumber\\
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{off}}&=
\tfrac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})e_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{off}},1)}.
\label{eq:power-mse}
\end{align}
The conditional terms keep errors during the sparse on state visible instead of allowing the many off samples to dominate the regression objective.

To preserve switching behaviour, define $\Delta y_{b,t,i}=y_{b,t,i}-y_{b,t-1,i}$ and $m_{b,t,i}=\max(z_{b,t,i},z_{b,t-1,i})$. With $N_i^{\Delta}=\sum_{b,t=2}^{T}m_{b,t,i}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\Delta}=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{b,\,t=2}^{T}m_{b,t,i}
(\Delta\hat{y}_{b,t,i}-\Delta y_{b,t,i})^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\Delta},1)}.
\label{eq:transition-loss}
\end{equation}
Window-level energy is measured in watts using $P_{b,t,i}=[\sigma_i y_{b,t,i}+\mu_i]_+$ and $\hat P_{b,t,i}=[\sigma_i\hat y_{b,t,i}+\mu_i]_+$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RE}_i=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{\bigl|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\hat{P}_{b,t,i}-P_{b,t,i})\bigr|}
{\sum_{t=1}^{T}P_{b,t,i}+TP_0},
\quad P_0=10~\mathrm{W}.
\label{eq:energy-loss}
\end{equation}
The per-appliance power loss is therefore
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}={}&
\mathrm{MSE}_i+\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{on}}
+0.5\,\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{off}}\\
&+0.15\,\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\Delta}+0.25\,\mathrm{RE}_i.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:power-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{State loss:}
Let $\hat s_{b,t,i}\in(0,1)$ denote the predicted on-state probability and $\pi_i$ the proportion of on samples for appliance $i$ in the training set. Its positive-class weight is $w_i=\min\{(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i,12\}$. The weighted binary cross-entropy is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{BCE}_i=-\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}\bigl[&
w_i z_{b,t,i}\log\hat s_{b,t,i}\\
&+(1-z_{b,t,i})\log(1-\hat s_{b,t,i})\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weighted-bce}
\end{equation}
An additional false-positive term penalizes high on probabilities when the appliance is off:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}=
\frac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})\hat s_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{off}},1)},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}=\mathrm{BCE}_i+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}.
\label{eq:state-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{Appliance-wise task balance:}
Because MSE and BCE have different numerical scales, the state loss of each appliance is matched to its own power loss. Let
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r&=\operatorname{sg}\!\left(
\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power}}}
{\sum_j\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},j}+\varepsilon}\right),\\
q_i&=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}}
{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}+\varepsilon},\qquad
r_i=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(q_i,\frac{r}{3},3r\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:balanced-ratio}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the weights and $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. Each appliance has its own power--state ratio, bounded relative to the joint ratio to limit extreme weights. The shared bounds retain some cross-appliance dependence; this is loss-scale balancing rather than direct gradient-norm equalization.

\subsection{Training Strategy}
\label{sec:training-strategy}

Training alternates between recorded and recomposed aggregate windows with equal probability. The recorded windows retain the natural joint distribution of household loads. The recomposed windows reduce dependence on appliance combinations and residual backgrounds observed in a particular house, following the use of synthetic aggregates in NILM~\cite{kelly2015neural,liu2026recomposition}. From each recorded training sequence, the non-target residual is
\begin{equation}
R_t=\left[x_t-\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{t,i}\right]_+.
\label{eq:training-residual}
\end{equation}
For a recomposed window, each appliance trace and the residual are drawn from independently selected training windows:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_t=R_t^{(k_0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\qquad
\tilde{P}_{t,i}=P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},\quad
\tilde{z}_{t,i}=z_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\label{eq:training-recomposition}
\end{equation}
where $k_0,k_1,\ldots,k_N$ denote independent window indices. The additive construction therefore preserves the power and state labels of every selected appliance.

To prevent sparse events from disappearing from the recomposed set, one focal appliance $i^\star$ is selected uniformly for each synthetic window and its trace is drawn from windows containing at least one on sample. The residual window is selected by first sampling uniformly from the non-empty median-power intervals $[0,100)$, $[100,200)$, $[200,400)$, $[400,800)$, and $[800,\infty)$~W, and then sampling a window within that interval. The remaining appliance traces are sampled without this constraint. Recomposition is used only for training; validation and test retain the recorded aggregate and its aligned labels. Fig.~\ref{fig:focal-mix} summarises the two-step procedure.

\begin{figure}[!t]

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocess}

Aggregate and appliance channels are independently mean-resampled onto an 8~s grid. The primary experiments retain the recorded timing; residual timing discrepancies remain a limitation of the measurements.

Let $\mu_x,\sigma_x$ and $\mu_i,\sigma_i$ be the aggregate and appliance statistics estimated from the training houses. The input and target powers are standardized as
\begin{equation}
\bar x_{n,t}=\frac{x_{n,t}-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\qquad
y_{n,t,i}=\frac{P_{n,t,i}-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}.
\label{eq:power-normalization}
\end{equation}

State labels are obtained before windowing from $z_{t,i}=\mathbb{I}[P_{t,i}\geq\tau_i]$, where $t=1,\ldots,T_{\mathrm{rec}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$ is the length of the recorded sequence. Off gaps no longer than $G_i$ are joined to the surrounding on period, and on periods shorter than $D_i$ are discarded. Algorithm~\ref{alg:labeling} gives the full procedure; Table~\ref{tab:label} lists the per-appliance parameters. A 30~s fridge gap is used in UK-DALE house~5. Isolated spikes are removed only for the appliances marked in Table~\ref{tab:label}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{On/Off State Labeling for Appliance $i$}
\label{alg:labeling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} $P_{1:T_{\mathrm{rec}}}$ — resampled power ($T_{\mathrm{rec}}$ = recording length); $\tau$ — on-power threshold; $G$ — min-off gap (samples); $D$ — min-on duration (samples)
\STATE \textbf{Output:} binary label sequence $z_{1:T_{\mathrm{rec}}}$
\IF{spike removal enabled for appliance $i$}
    \STATE Remove isolated outliers from $P$ via rolling-window $z$-statistic
\ENDIF
\FOR{$t=1$ \textbf{to} $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$}
    \STATE $z_t \leftarrow \mathbb{I}[P_t \geq \tau]$ \COMMENT{threshold}
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each OFF run $[s,\,e)$ in $z$}
    \IF{$e - s \leq G$}
        \STATE $z_s,\ldots,z_{e-1} \leftarrow 1$ \COMMENT{close short gap}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each \emph{complete} ON run $[s,\,e)$ in $z$ (not at segment boundary)}
    \IF{$e - s < D$}
        \STATE $z_s,\ldots,z_{e-1} \leftarrow 0$ \COMMENT{remove brief event}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $z_{1:T_{\mathrm{rec}}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

MART-NILM is compared with UNet-NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet}, MAT-Conv~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}, and TransferNILM~\cite{sun2025transfer}. Each baseline retains the architecture, objective, and training hyperparameters of its source paper, while all models use the common house-held-out data protocol. Table~\ref{tab:cost} lists their settings. All four models are trained with Adam. UNet-NILM runs for the full 50 epochs. MAT-Conv stops after 30 epochs without improvement. TransferNILM jointly minimizes mean-squared power error and binary cross-entropy state error; it runs for at most 100 epochs and stops after 60 epochs without improvement in its published validation criterion. MART-NILM uses weight decay $10^{-4}$ and unit-norm gradient clipping. Its learning rate is reduced by a factor of 0.5 after 12 epochs without validation improvement, to a minimum of $10^{-5}$. Training is limited to 150 epochs; early stopping is disabled before epoch 100 and thereafter uses a patience of 55 epochs. A 1024-sample window covers about 136.5~min.

For MART-NILM, checkpoint selection and state calibration use only the validation houses. Average precision (AP) is computed from the continuous state probabilities, and the retained checkpoint minimizes
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{val}}=\mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{val}}
+1-\mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{macro}}_{\mathrm{val}},
\label{eq:checkpoint-score}
\end{equation}
where the AP term summarizes the precision--recall curve without selecting a
decision threshold. After checkpoint selection,
an appliance-specific threshold is chosen from $\{0.05,0.06,\ldots,0.98\}$ to
maximize validation F1. For the microwave, predicted on periods shorter than
32~s are removed and off gaps no longer than 24~s are joined. The resulting
binary state $\hat z_{t,i}$ is also applied to the power output,
$\hat P^{\mathrm{eval}}_{t,i}=\hat z_{t,i}\hat P_{t,i}$. The fitted thresholds
and temporal rules are then frozen and applied unchanged to both test houses.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Each appliance is evaluated on power and on the on--off state. For an evaluated record of length $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$, $P_{t}$ is the recorded power in watts and $\hat{P}_{t}$ is the predicted power. The label $z_{t}$ equals 1 when the appliance is recorded on and 0 when it is off. $\hat{z}_{t}$ is the predicted label. The Average column in Table~\ref{tab:results} is the mean of the five appliance scores.

For an appliance-level score $q_{h,i}$ of appliance $i$ in house $h$, we first form the appliance average within that house and then give every house equal weight,
\begin{equation}
\bar q_h=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{h,i},\qquad
\bar q_{\mathcal H}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal H|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\bar q_h.
\label{eq:house-macro}
\end{equation}
For F1, $\bar q_h$ is the macro-F1 over the five appliances. Checkpoint selection uses $\mathcal H=\{\text{UK-DALE~5},\text{REFIT~2}\}$, the two validation houses. UK-DALE~2 and REFIT~20 are excluded from checkpoint selection and reported separately as cross-house test cases. Their equal-house mean is used for the architecture-ablation summary. Because these test cases were also inspected during method development, they constitute a development-informed benchmark rather than an untouched confirmatory evaluation~\cite{cawley2010overfitting}. Three-seed variability measures training randomness on this fixed split, not variability across households.

\subsubsection{Power}
Power error is reported using MAE, ON MAE, and SAE. MAE uses every sample, whereas ON MAE is restricted to samples with $z_t=1$ so that reconstruction quality during appliance operation remains visible.
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{rec}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{rec}}}\left|P_{t}-\hat{P}_{t}\right|.
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
SAE divides the record into consecutive blocks of $L=1200$ samples, or 160~min at 8~s. The index of a block is $k$, and $K=\lfloor T_{\mathrm{rec}}/L\rfloor$ is the number of complete blocks. A trailing block shorter than $L$ is omitted. SAE is the mean absolute error of the average power in each block,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SAE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=(k-1)L+1}^{kL}\left(P_{t}-\hat{P}_{t}\right)\right|.
\label{eq:sae}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{On-Off State}
The recorded label $z_t$ follows the rules in Table~\ref{tab:label}, and $\hat{z}_t$ is the predicted binary state. For each appliance, $\mathrm{TP}$ (true positives) counts samples correctly identified as on ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=1$), $\mathrm{FP}$ (false positives) counts inactive samples falsely detected as on ($z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=1$), and $\mathrm{FN}$ (false negatives) counts active samples missed as off ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=0$):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
Precision is the fraction of predicted on samples that are truly on, and recall is the fraction of actual on samples detected. F1 is their harmonic mean; true negatives ($\mathrm{TN}$, $z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=0$) are excluded to prevent long idle background periods from artificially inflating the score.
To evaluate state ranking independently of the calibrated decision threshold, we also report AP from the continuous state probability. Let $\mathcal{P}_n$ and $\mathcal{R}_n$ denote precision and recall after the $n$th distinct probability threshold, with $\mathcal{R}_0=0$. The non-interpolated AP is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP}=\sum_n\left(\mathcal{R}_n-\mathcal{R}_{n-1}\right)\mathcal{P}_n.
\label{eq:average-precision}
\end{equation}
Unlike F1, AP does not use the appliance-specific threshold or the temporal state postprocessing fitted on the validation houses.

The multi-scale CNN is reduced to its central kernel while preserving its
width and residual path.  Removing the feature encoder retains only a
pointwise channel projection, and removing the temporal encoder uses an
identity mapping.  The appliance-decoder ablation sends the shared temporal
representation directly to five independent power/state readouts.  The final
variant retains these appliance-specific readouts but removes information
exchange between them.  These replacements preserve the joint prediction
task and isolate the contribution of each top-level module.

The shared temporal encoder gives the largest contribution: its removal
increases MAE by 1.44~W and ON MAE by 45.13~W, while macro-F1 falls from
0.796 to 0.710.  Removing the appliance-specific decoders increases ON MAE
by 22.90~W and reduces macro-F1 by 0.013.  The feature encoder provides a
smaller but consistent benefit to ON-state reconstruction and detection.
The multi-scale branches have little effect on the aggregate averages, but their
benefit is concentrated on the sparse microwave task: on validation
UK-DALE~5 the single-scale variant increases microwave ON MAE from 589.49 to 696.45~W,

also exhibits a task trade-off.  Removing it lowers average ON MAE, but
reduces macro-F1 and lowers REFIT~20 microwave F1 from 0.549 to 0.496.
Waveform inspection agrees with the numerical results: removing temporal
encoding produces prolonged false fridge activations and poorly aligned
microwave plateaus, while removing the appliance decoders increases
fragmentation and amplitude error.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented MART-NILM for joint appliance power and state estimation from low-frequency aggregate measurements. Under the common five-week house-held-out benchmark, it achieves the lowest appliance-average power error and highest macro-F1 and average precision on both test houses among the reproduced models, using 1.373 million parameters. The shared temporal encoder and appliance-specific decoders provide the principal architectural gains. Focal-event mixing improves event coverage, while bounded appliance-wise balancing addresses unequal power--state loss scales; their combination improves validation detection without additional inference computation. The ablations also expose trade-offs, including limited microwave detection gains from the training strategies. These findings support compact joint estimation as a basis for residential load observability. Evidence remains restricted to two test houses inspected during development; broader household generalisation and downstream energy-management benefits require independent evaluation.
