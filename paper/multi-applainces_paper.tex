\section{Introduction}

Reducing residential carbon footprints and curbing greenhouse gas emissions depend on actionable, fine-grained visibility into household electricity consumption~\cite{wang2026smartmeter}. Although direct sub-metering can provide itemized feedback to guide conservation, installing dedicated hardware sensors on every appliance is intrusive, expensive, and difficult to scale~\cite{luo2025scaling}. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) addresses this bottleneck by estimating the power and the on--off state of individual appliances from a single aggregate meter, without a sensor on each appliance. A recent systematic review groups more than thirty years of work into deterministic low-frequency methods, probabilistic and optimization models, and deep networks, and finds that reported accuracy depends as much on the sensing setup and the evaluation protocol as on the model~\cite{dasilva2026nilm}. In homes, deep learning is now the usual approach, while data requirements, real-time use, and interpretability remain open~\cite{rafiq2024nilm}.

In conventional single-appliance NILM, a separate network is trained for each appliance. While single-appliance models dominate the existing literature, their linear scaling in computational footprint and memory storage limits real-world smart-meter deployment, leading recent reviews to highlight multi-appliance NILM as a crucial future research direction~\cite{rafiq2024nilm,dasilva2026nilm}. By mapping one aggregate window to several loads simultaneously within a single network, multi-appliance NILM provides constant inference overhead and preserves joint operational context~\cite{faustine2020unet,li2023onemany}. However, it remains far less explored due to optimization challenges such as gradient conflict and cross-appliance scale imbalance~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}. Furthermore, existing multi-appliance designs typically adopt a shared feature extractor that merely feeds into separate output heads, whereas the truly beneficial form of joint disaggregation is capturing explicit relational dependencies among appliances.

A joint network is not, by itself, evidence about an unseen home. The measured aggregate is
\begin{equation}
x_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}+b_{t}+\epsilon_{t},
\label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is time, $i=1,\ldots,M$ indexes the target appliances, $M$ is their number, $P_{t,i}$ is the power of appliance $i$, $b_{t}$ is the unmonitored background, and $\epsilon_{t}$ collects measurement error and timing inconsistency. The background includes every load outside the target set. A model can treat patterns in $b_{t}$ as evidence for a target that merely co-occurs with them~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. This term is not a small disturbance: it can be far larger than the roughly 80~W demand of a fridge.
Two problems follow. A rare on event may be seen only against the backgrounds of the training houses, so the network can treat that background as evidence for the appliance. Joint training creates a second problem. For one appliance, power is a regression loss and the on--off state is a classification loss. The two losses do not share a scale, and the scale is different for each appliance. One ratio taken over all appliances would multiply every classification loss by the same number, so a large regression error on the microwave would change the weight of the fridge's classification error.

We propose MART-NILM, a multi-appliance relational temporal network that reads the aggregate and estimates the five appliances together. During training, a real on event is placed against different levels of the remaining household load. The recorded meter is left unchanged at test time. For each appliance, the regression loss and the classification loss are placed on one scale and added. The training objective is the sum of these appliance losses. Training, validation, and test use different houses.

The contributions are threefold. First, training, validation, and test use different houses, the test meter keeps its recorded residual, and five appliances are scored together on UK-DALE and REFIT. Second, training shows rare on events against a range of the remaining household load, and this step adds nothing at test time. Third, the objective is the sum over appliances of a regression loss and a classification loss. The classification loss of each appliance is scaled by that appliance's own ratio of the two losses.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $x_t$ denote aggregate power and $P_{t,i}$ the power of target appliance $i$, as in \eqref{eq:aggregate}. From a standardized aggregate window $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$, the model jointly estimates the standardized power and on-state probability of $M=5$ appliances,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times M},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}\in[0,1]^{T\times M}.
\label{eq:outputs}
\end{equation}
The input contains no appliance measurements or hand-crafted channels. The unobserved remainder is
\begin{equation}
x_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}=b_{t}+\epsilon_{t}.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
Hence the network must distinguish appliance activity from both other targets and unmonitored household demand.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/multinilm_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture of MART-NILM. The aggregate input is processed by a multi-scale convolutional stem and a shared temporal encoder. Appliance-specific decoders form five feature streams, cross-appliance attention exchanges information among them, and separate power and state heads jointly produce gated power estimates and on--off probabilities.}
    \label{fig:multinilm-architecture}
\end{figure*}

MART-NILM follows the shared--private design in Fig.~\ref{fig:multinilm-architecture}. A shared encoder extracts evidence once from the aggregate; appliance-specific decoders then separate this evidence into five streams; finally, relation attention allows the streams to exchange information before joint power and state prediction. Temporal resolution is preserved throughout.

\textit{Shared aggregate encoder:}
The encoder combines a multi-branch convolutional stem~\cite{szegedy2015inception} with residual temporal convolution. Parallel kernels capture switching patterns at complementary local scales. In the shallow encoder, instance--batch normalization divides the channels between instance and batch normalization~\cite{pan2018ibn}, reducing house-specific variation while retaining amplitude information. The resulting sequence is processed by five dilated temporal blocks~\cite{lea2017tcn}. Writing $\mathcal{T}_{\ell}$ for the normalized, activated convolution in block $\ell$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)}
=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}+\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
\!\left(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)};d_{\ell}\right),
\qquad d_{\ell}\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}.
\label{eq:temporal-block}
\end{equation}
This produces the shared representation $\mathbf{H}$ without temporal downsampling, preserving the alignment between input transitions and appliance outputs.

\textit{Appliance decoding and relation modelling:}
Each decoder applies channel recalibration~\cite{hu2018senet} and a local residual transformation to obtain an appliance-specific representation $\mathbf{F}_i$. These private streams prevent all appliances from being forced into the same representation. To model simultaneous operation, scaled dot-product attention~\cite{vaswani2017attention} is evaluated across appliances at each time $t$, rather than across time:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_t=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}_t\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt{d}}
\right),\qquad
\mathbf{M}_t=\mathbf{A}_t\mathbf{V}_t.
\label{eq:relation-attention}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{Q}_t$, $\mathbf{K}_t$, and $\mathbf{V}_t$ are learned projections of the stacked appliance representations, and $d$ is their projection width. A gated residual connection adds the relation message $\mathbf{M}_t$ to each private stream, so cross-appliance context supplements rather than replaces appliance-specific evidence.

\textit{Joint power and state prediction:}
For each appliance, two time-shared linear heads produce a raw standardized power $\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)$ and a state logit $a_i(t)$. The on-state probability $\hat p_i(t)=\sigma(a_i(t))$ is both the state prediction and a differentiable gate on the power branch~\cite{shin2019sgn,xiong2024matnilm}:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_i(t)=\hat p_i(t)\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)
+[1-\hat p_i(t)]y_i^{\mathrm{off}},
\label{eq:soft-power-gate}
\end{equation}
where $y_i^{\mathrm{off}}=-\mu_i/\sigma_i$ is zero watts on the standardized scale. Thus an on probability near one preserves the regressed waveform, whereas a probability near zero moves the estimate towards zero watts. The final prediction in physical units is
\begin{equation}
\hat P_i(t)=\left[\sigma_i\hat y_i(t)+\mu_i\right]_+,
\qquad [u]_+=\max(u,0).
\label{eq:power-in-watts}
\end{equation}
The state head therefore provides an explicit operating-state prior while the power head retains the appliance waveform.

\subsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss}

The model is trained jointly over all appliances, as in multi-task NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget}. Let $b=1,\ldots,B$ index windows in a mini-batch, $t=1,\ldots,T$ index samples, and $i=1,\ldots,M$ index appliances. The standardized target and predicted powers are $y_{b,t,i}$ and $\hat y_{b,t,i}$; $z_{b,t,i}\in\{0,1\}$ is the on--off label; and $\hat p_{b,t,i}\in(0,1)$ is the predicted on probability. The objective separates power regression from state classification:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power}}}
+\lambda\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{M}r_i\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state}}},
\qquad \lambda=0.8.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Hence every appliance contributes one power loss and one state loss, and the five appliance losses are summed to train a single network.

\textit{Power loss:}
For appliance $i$, let $e_{b,t,i}=\hat y_{b,t,i}-y_{b,t,i}$, $N_i^{\mathrm{on}}=\sum_{b,t}z_{b,t,i}$, and $N_i^{\mathrm{off}}=BT-N_i^{\mathrm{on}}$. The overall, on-state, and off-state mean squared errors are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{MSE}_i&=\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}e_{b,t,i}^{2},\nonumber\\
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{on}}&=
\frac{\sum_{b,t}z_{b,t,i}e_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{on}},1)},\nonumber\\
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{off}}&=
\frac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})e_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{off}},1)}.
\label{eq:power-mse}
\end{align}
The conditional terms keep errors during the sparse on state visible instead of allowing the many off samples to dominate the regression objective.

To preserve switching behaviour, define $\Delta y_{b,t,i}=y_{b,t,i}-y_{b,t-1,i}$ and $m_{b,t,i}=\max(z_{b,t,i},z_{b,t-1,i})$. With $N_i^{\Delta}=\sum_{b,t=2}^{T}m_{b,t,i}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\Delta}=
\frac{\sum_{b,t=2}^{T}m_{b,t,i}
(\Delta\hat y_{b,t,i}-\Delta y_{b,t,i})^2}
{\max(N_i^{\Delta},1)}.
\label{eq:transition-loss}
\end{equation}
Window-level energy is measured in watts using $P_{b,t,i}=[\sigma_i y_{b,t,i}+\mu_i]_+$ and $\hat P_{b,t,i}=[\sigma_i\hat y_{b,t,i}+\mu_i]_+$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RE}_i=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\hat P_{b,t,i}-P_{b,t,i})\right|}
{\sum_{t=1}^{T}P_{b,t,i}+TP_0},
\qquad P_0=10~\mathrm{W}.
\label{eq:energy-loss}
\end{equation}
The per-appliance power loss is therefore
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}={}&
\mathrm{MSE}_i+\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{on}}
+0.5\,\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{off}}\\
&+0.15\,\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\Delta}+0.25\,\mathrm{RE}_i.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:power-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{State loss:}
Let $\pi_i$ denote the proportion of on samples for appliance $i$ in the training set. Its positive-class weight is $w_i=\min\{(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i,12\}$. The weighted binary cross-entropy is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{BCE}_i=-\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}\bigl[&
w_i z_{b,t,i}\log\hat p_{b,t,i}\\
&+(1-z_{b,t,i})\log(1-\hat p_{b,t,i})\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weighted-bce}
\end{equation}
An additional false-positive term penalizes high on probabilities when the appliance is off:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}=
\frac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})\hat p_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{off}},1)},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}=\mathrm{BCE}_i+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}.
\label{eq:state-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{Appliance-wise task balance:}
Because MSE and BCE have different numerical scales, the state loss of each appliance is matched to its own power loss. Let
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r&=\operatorname{sg}\!\left(
\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power}}}
{\sum_j\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},j}+\varepsilon}\right),\\
q_i&=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}}
{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}+\varepsilon},\qquad
r_i=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(q_i,\frac{r}{3},3r\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:balanced-ratio}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the weights and $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. Substitution of $r_i$ into Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} yields the complete multi-appliance objective while preventing one appliance from setting the classification scale of all others.

\subsection{Training Strategy}
\label{sec:training-strategy}

Training alternates between recorded and recomposed aggregate windows with equal probability. The recorded windows retain the natural joint distribution of household loads. The recomposed windows reduce dependence on appliance combinations and residual backgrounds observed in a particular house, following the use of synthetic aggregates in NILM~\cite{kelly2015neural,liu2026recomposition}. From each recorded training sequence, the non-target residual is
\begin{equation}
R_t=\left[x_t-\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}\right]_+.
\label{eq:training-residual}
\end{equation}
For a recomposed window, each appliance trace and the residual are drawn from independently selected training windows:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_t=R_t^{(k_0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\qquad
\tilde{P}_{t,i}=P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},\quad
\tilde{z}_{t,i}=z_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\label{eq:training-recomposition}
\end{equation}
where $k_0,k_1,\ldots,k_M$ denote independent window indices. The additive construction therefore preserves the power and state labels of every selected appliance.

To prevent sparse events from disappearing from the recomposed set, one focal appliance $i^\star$ is selected uniformly for each synthetic window and its trace is drawn from windows containing at least one on sample. The residual window is selected by first sampling uniformly from the non-empty median-power intervals $[0,100)$, $[100,200)$, $[200,400)$, $[400,800)$, and $[800,\infty)$~W, and then sampling a window within that interval. The remaining appliance traces are sampled without this constraint. Recomposition is used only for training; validation and test retain the recorded aggregate and its aligned labels.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocess}

Aggregate and appliance channels are independently mean-resampled onto an 8~s grid. The primary experiments retain the recorded timing; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-alignment} reports a separate timing-sensitivity audit that was not used for model selection.

Let $\mu_x,\sigma_x$ and $\mu_i,\sigma_i$ be the aggregate and appliance statistics estimated from the training houses. The input and target powers are standardized as
\begin{equation}
\bar x_{n,t}=\frac{x_{n,t}-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\qquad
y_{n,t,i}=\frac{P_{n,t,i}-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}.
\label{eq:power-normalization}
\end{equation}

Model selection and state calibration use only the validation houses. The retained checkpoint minimizes
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{val}}=\mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{val}}
+1-\mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{macro}}_{\mathrm{val}},
\label{eq:checkpoint-score}
\end{equation}
where AP is computed from the continuous state probabilities. After checkpoint selection, an appliance-specific threshold is chosen from $\{0.05,0.06,\ldots,0.98\}$ to maximize validation F1. For the microwave, predicted on periods shorter than 32~s are removed and off gaps no longer than 24~s are joined. The resulting binary state $\hat z_{t,i}$ is also applied to the power output, $\hat P^{\mathrm{eval}}_{t,i}=\hat z_{t,i}\hat P_{t,i}$. The fitted thresholds and temporal rules are then frozen and applied unchanged to both test houses.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Each appliance is evaluated on power and on the on--off state. For an evaluated record of length $T$, $P_{t}$ is the recorded power in watts and $\hat{P}_{t}$ is the predicted power. The label $z_{t}$ equals 1 when the appliance is recorded on and 0 when it is off. $\hat{z}_{t}$ is the predicted label. The Average column in Table~\ref{tab:results} is the mean of the five appliance scores. Here $T$ is the length of the record, not the training window of Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

For an appliance-level score $q_{h,i}$ of appliance $i$ in house $h$, we first form the appliance average within that house and then give every house equal weight,
\begin{equation}
\bar q_h=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{h,i},\qquad
\bar q_{\mathcal H}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal H|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\bar q_h.
\label{eq:house-macro}
\end{equation}
For F1, $\bar q_h$ is the macro-F1 over the five appliances. The ablation tables use $\mathcal H=\{\text{UK-DALE~5},\text{REFIT~2}\}$, the two validation houses. This separation prevents repeated design choices from biasing the final evaluation~\cite{cawley2010overfitting}. UK-DALE~2 and REFIT~20 are excluded from model selection and are reported separately as cross-house test cases; their equal-house mean is included only as a summary and never replaces the two house-specific results.

\subsubsection{Power}
The same absolute error is reported in three ways. MAE uses every sample. ON MAE uses samples with $z_{t}=1$. OFF MAE uses samples with $z_{t}=0$.
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|P_{t}-\hat{P}_{t}\right|.
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
SAE divides the record into consecutive blocks of $L=1200$ samples, or 160~min at 8~s. The index of a block is $k$, and $K=\lfloor T/L\rfloor$ is the number of complete blocks. A trailing block shorter than $L$ is omitted. SAE is the mean absolute error of the average power in each block,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SAE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=(k-1)L+1}^{kL}\left(P_{t}-\hat{P}_{t}\right)\right|.
\label{eq:sae}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{On-Off State}
The recorded label $z_t$ follows the rules in Table~\ref{tab:label}, and $\hat{z}_t$ is the predicted binary state. For each appliance, $\mathrm{TP}$ (true positives) counts samples correctly identified as on ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=1$), $\mathrm{FP}$ (false positives) counts inactive samples falsely detected as on ($z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=1$), and $\mathrm{FN}$ (false negatives) counts active samples missed as off ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=0$):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
Precision is the fraction of predicted on samples that are truly on, and recall is the fraction of actual on samples detected. F1 is their harmonic mean; true negatives ($\mathrm{TN}$, $z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=0$) are excluded to prevent long idle background periods from artificially inflating the score.

The state--power gate has the largest effect: removing it increases average
MAE by 5.97~W and SAE by 3.82~W. Dilated temporal modelling, the local
decoder, and relation attention each improve macro-F1 relative to their
ablations. Channel attention is not supported by the aggregate scores: its
removal gives the lowest MAE and highest macro-F1, although the difference is
small relative to the variation across seeds. The single-scale stem similarly
gives the lowest SAE without improving macro-F1. These two components are
therefore treated as candidates for further simplification rather than as
independently established sources of improvement.
