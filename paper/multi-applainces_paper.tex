\begin{abstract}
Appliance-level visibility supports residential demand-side management, but aggregate meters do not directly reveal individual consumption or operating states. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) estimates these quantities without dedicated sensors. Deep multi-appliance NILM uses one neural model to jointly estimate appliance power and multi-label ON-OFF states from aggregate measurements, but sparse activations under limited background conditions and unequal power-state loss scales complicate training. This paper proposes the Multi-Appliance Relational Temporal Network for NILM (MART-NILM), a compact estimator with a shared temporal convolutional encoder, appliance-specific decoders, and relational attention. Focal-event background-stratified mixing varies background demand around active appliance traces, while bounded appliance-wise balancing calibrates each state loss relative to its corresponding power loss. Both operate only during training. On two public residential datasets with disjoint training, validation, and test houses, MART-NILM attains appliance-average mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 7.3 and 10.9 W and macro-F1 scores of 0.88 and 0.72, outperforming three reproduced baselines. With 1.373 million parameters, it also records lower observed training and inference times under the evaluated protocol. These results support compact joint power-state estimation for residential load monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Effective residential demand-side management requires visibility into the electricity consumption and operating status of individual appliances. Power estimates quantify appliance demand, whereas state estimates expose activation timing; aggregate metering reveals neither directly. Appliance-level feedback can support energy conservation~\cite{delmas2013information}, but dedicated submetering requires installing and maintaining additional sensors. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) commonly estimates both appliance power consumption and operating status from aggregate measurements, avoiding the need for dedicated appliance-level submetering~\cite{gopinath2020energy}. Joint estimation of these outputs is important because a low average power error can still mask missed operating periods, especially for sparsely active appliances.

Classical NILM identifies appliance signatures and infers appliance states using finite-state or factorial hidden Markov models~\cite{hart1992nilm,kolter2012fhmm}. Deep approaches instead learn representations directly from aggregate windows~\cite{kelly2015neural}, reducing the need to specify signatures and state-transition models manually. Regardless of the method used, the aggregate power measured at the main meter includes demand from loads outside the monitored target set. For $N$ target appliances, the measured power is
\begin{equation}
x_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_{t,i} + b_t + \epsilon_t,
\label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $P_{t,i}$ is target power, $b_t$ is background demand from unmonitored loads, and $\epsilon_t$ represents measurement and timing discrepancies. Across houses, the background demand $b_t$ may exceed the power of a low-power target appliance and may follow a different distribution. Consequently, a model trained in one house may encounter unfamiliar background demand in another, even when the target appliance is unchanged. Such cross-house variation makes NILM challenging because the target appliance must be distinguished from unfamiliar background demand.

Against this background, deep NILM is commonly formulated as either single-appliance or multi-appliance estimation. Single-appliance networks train one model for each appliance and may yield better appliance-level disaggregation accuracy in some settings~\cite{wang2022transformer,petralia2025nilmformer}, but repeating a model for every load duplicates aggregate feature extraction and storage, which is undesirable for smart-meter or home-gateway deployment. Recent deep NILM work has therefore increasingly studied multi-appliance estimation, in which one model jointly estimates the power traces and ON-state probabilities of a fixed set of $N$ target appliances from the same aggregate window~\cite{faustine2020unet,alami2023convnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget,sun2025transfer}. NILM research commonly considers two complementary tasks: power estimation and ON-OFF state detection.

Joint multi-appliance and multi-label estimation is more challenging than running $N$ independent predictors. Concurrent activations provide useful context, but superposed transitions and background demand can mask weak targets~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. Therefore, a multi-appliance model must distinguish appliance-specific signatures from overlapping demand, while exploiting relationships among concurrently active appliances and producing consistent power and state predictions.

Two further challenges arise when training a joint multi-appliance power-state model. First, sparse appliance activations in the available training data are observed under only a subset of background-demand conditions. The model may therefore use background-demand correlations as shortcuts instead of learning the appliance signature; such shortcuts can fail when those correlations change across households~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. Second, appliance power ranges and ON-state frequencies differ substantially. Existing joint objectives use a shared regression-classification balance, either as a fixed coefficient or a global loss-ratio weight~\cite{sun2025transfer,liu2022samnet}. Such a shared balance cannot represent appliance-specific scales; when derived from joint losses, a large power error for one appliance also changes the relative state weight of the others. These observations motivate controlling background-demand conditions and balancing the power and state objectives separately for each appliance.

To address these issues, we propose the Multi-Appliance Relational Temporal Network for NILM (MART-NILM), a relational temporal model for joint power and ON-OFF state estimation under varying background demand.

The main contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A compact multi-appliance architecture, MART-NILM, is designed for
    joint power and state estimation. A shared convolutional temporal encoder
    avoids duplicating the temporal backbone for each appliance, while
    appliance-specific decoders and cross-appliance attention retain appliance
    specialization and relational context. The model produces five power-state
    output pairs in a single forward pass with $1.373$ million parameters.

    \item Two training-only methods address two distinct training challenges.
    First, focal-event background-stratified mixing (hereafter focal-event mixing) addresses sparse appliance
    activations and limited background-demand coverage by pairing selected ON
    events with diverse recorded background demand. Second, bounded
    appliance-wise balancing addresses appliance-specific power-state loss
    imbalance by separately calibrating the power and state objectives for each
    appliance. Neither method adds inference-time computation.

    \item A cross-house evaluation is conducted using aggregate power
    recordings from two public residential
    datasets. Baseline comparisons are repeated over three random seeds.
    Architecture and training-strategy ablations, together with analyses
    stratified by background demand, assess power-estimation and state-detection
    performance, computational cost, and residual failure modes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

Joint multi-appliance power-state estimation has a nested multi-task structure: power regression and state classification are coupled within each appliance, while representations are shared across appliances. Related formulations have been described as ``multi-task-multi-appliance''~\cite{faustine2020unet} and ``multi-appliance-task''~\cite{xiong2024matnilm,sun2025transfer}. From a multi-task learning (MTL) perspective~\cite{vandenhende2022survey}, this setting requires decisions about both output coupling and representation sharing. The following review considers appliance-head design, cross-appliance body design, and training strategies for joint power-state estimation.

\subsection{Appliance-Head Design}
\label{sec:related-head}

Relevant NILM studies use convolutional neural networks (CNNs)~\cite{faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm}, recurrent neural networks (RNNs)~\cite{murray2019transferability}, temporal convolutional networks (TCNs)~\cite{alami2023convnilm,dash2024jestie}, and Transformers~\cite{sun2025transfer} as temporal backbones. Beyond backbone choice, appliance-head design determines how power and state outputs are coupled for each appliance. Figure~\ref{fig:appliance-head} summarizes three such mechanisms.

Murray \emph{et al.}~\cite{murray2019transferability} concatenate predicted states with power features (Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(a)), but the regressor remains free to produce nonzero power for an OFF prediction. The subtask-gated network (SGN)~\cite{shin2019sgn} instead gates regression outputs with ON-state probabilities (Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(b)), suppressing inactive power while making reconstruction sensitive to missed activations. The scale- and context-aware network (SCANet)~\cite{chen2021sca} adds parallel dilated branches and self-attention (Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(c)), increasing processing within each target model. These methods couple the two tasks, but their single-appliance formulations require separate models for different targets, repeating aggregate feature extraction without explicit cross-appliance feature exchange.

\subsection{Cross-Appliance Body Design}
\label{sec:related-body}

Cross-appliance body design determines which representations remain shared, which are decoded separately, and where appliance-specific streams exchange information. Figure~\ref{fig:model-body-design} summarizes these mechanisms. Multi-Appliance-Task NILM (MATNilm)~\cite{xiong2024matnilm} reduces this duplication through shared encoding and private appliance decoders (Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(a)). Its temporal and appliance-wise attention captures both dependencies, but repeats temporal attention and feed-forward processing in the private branches. Sun \emph{et al.}~\cite{sun2025transfer} use the same sharing pattern with Transformer blocks in shared and appliance-specific layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(a)). Downsampling reduces shared attention cost, but temporal attention is still repeated for individual appliances. Both support joint multi-appliance power and state estimation; their private sequence-processing blocks nevertheless add computation beyond the shared encoder.

UNet-NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet} follows the shared-body/task-head pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(b)), predicting multiple appliance powers and states from common features. It avoids private temporal blocks, but leaves appliance differentiation to shared features and output heads, without dedicated appliance-specific decoder streams or explicit exchange between such streams.

Cross-appliance interaction (Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(c)) complements these designs by exchanging context among specialized representations. MATNilm already uses appliance-wise attention for this purpose~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}, while message passing provides a related mechanism in dense prediction~\cite{xu2018padnet}. These designs motivate examining how appliance-specific decoding and relation attention can be retained while reducing repeated temporal processing.

\subsection{Training Strategies for Joint Power-State Estimation}
\label{sec:related-training}

Training a joint multi-appliance power-state model also requires sufficient exposure to appliance activations under different background-demand conditions. Neural NILM constructs synthetic aggregates to increase training diversity~\cite{kelly2015neural}, SCANet augments ON-state examples~\cite{chen2021sca}, and aggregate recomposition varies background demand while preserving target labels~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. These approaches address limited training coverage, but input diversity alone does not ensure that sparse ON events are sampled across a controlled range of recorded background demand. This distinction matters when event-background correlations change across houses.

A separate issue is the unequal scale of power and state losses. Gradient normalization (GradNorm) balances task updates in generic MTL~\cite{chen2018gradnorm}, whereas SAMNet adapts a power-state loss ratio for joint NILM~\cite{liu2022samnet}. In a multi-appliance setting, a balance derived from aggregate losses may be driven by appliances with larger regression losses and may not reflect the power-state loss ratio of each appliance. This motivates appliance-wise balancing with bounds that limit extreme weights.

Together, these studies motivate the architectural and training choices examined in this paper. A shared temporal encoder, appliance-specific decoders, and relation attention address representation design, while focal-event mixing and bounded appliance-wise balancing address background-demand coverage and appliance-specific power-state loss scales, respectively.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Following Equation~\eqref{eq:aggregate}, let $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ denote an aggregate window of length $T$. The problem is to jointly estimate the power traces and ON-state probabilities of $N$ target appliances:
\begin{equation}
\left(\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\hat{\mathbf{S}}\right)=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N},\quad
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in[0,1]^{T\times N}
\label{eq:joint-estimation}
\end{equation}
Here $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ denotes the model parameters, and $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ collect the estimated power traces and ON-state probabilities, respectively. To make the multi-appliance and multi-label output structure explicit, the time-$t$ output slice of \eqref{eq:joint-estimation} is
\begin{equation}
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta},t}(\mathbf{x})=
\underbrace{\bigl(\hat y_{t,1},\hat s_{t,1},\ldots,
\hat y_{t,N},\hat s_{t,N}\bigr)}_{N\text{ appliance power-state pairs}},
\quad t=1,\ldots,T.
\label{eq:paired-outputs}
\end{equation}
Thus, for appliance $i$ at time $t$, $\hat y_{t,i}$ is the estimated standardized power and $\hat s_{t,i}\in[0,1]$ is its ON-state probability. Equations~\eqref{eq:joint-estimation} and~\eqref{eq:paired-outputs} define the objective: from one aggregate window, jointly recover the power trace and ON-state probability of every target appliance at every time step. Power estimation is a regression task, whereas state detection is multi-label classification because multiple appliances may be ON simultaneously. Binary ON-OFF labels summarize activity without resolving internal operating modes.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The implementation uses instance-batch normalization (IBN), batch normalization (BN), the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and fully connected (FC) output layers.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/martnilm_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Model architecture of MART-NILM.}
    \label{fig:martnilm-architecture}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:martnilm-architecture} presents MART-NILM, combining the private-stream interaction body in Fig.~\ref{fig:model-body-design}(c) with the state-gated heads in Fig.~\ref{fig:appliance-head}(b). The components are described below in processing order, using the same names as the architecture ablations in Table~\ref{tab:architecture-ablation}.

\textbf{Multi-scale CNN:}
Parallel convolutional branches~\cite{szegedy2015inception} extract local features at complementary time scales from the standardized aggregate $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}=\mathcal{F}\!\left(
[\mathcal{B}_{3}(\bar{\mathbf{x}});\mathcal{B}_{5}(\bar{\mathbf{x}});
\mathcal{B}_{9}(\bar{\mathbf{x}})]\right)+\mathcal{S}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}).
\label{eq:multiscale-fusion}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{B}_{k}$ is a kernel-$k$ convolution followed by IBN~\cite{pan2018ibn} and ReLU; $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation. The $1\times1$ fusion $\mathcal{F}$ combines the branches, while the projected shortcut $\mathcal{S}$ retains a direct input path. IBN applies instance normalization (IN) and BN to separate channel halves.

\textbf{Feature Encoder:}
Two convolution-IBN-ReLU stages combine the local features into a shared representation $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times T}$. Feature extraction is performed once for all appliances.

\textbf{Shared Temporal Encoder:}
Residual dilated TCN blocks~\cite{lea2017tcn} extend temporal context:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)}=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}+
\mathcal{T}_{2^{\ell}}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}),
\qquad \ell\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}.
\label{eq:temporal-block}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{T}_{d}$ comprises a dilation-$d$ convolution, BN, ReLU, and dropout. Increasing dilation expands context, while residual connections and symmetric padding retain the incoming features and temporal alignment. The output is $\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}^{(5)}$.

\textbf{Appliance Decoders:}
Each decoder applies a channel gate, inspired by channel recalibration~\cite{hu2018senet}, and a local residual transformation to obtain an appliance-specific representation $\mathbf{F}_i$. These private streams adapt the shared temporal features to individual load characteristics.

\textbf{Cross-Appliance Relation Attention:}
The relation module draws on the cross-task information-sharing principle of the Multi-Tasks Guided Prediction-and-Distillation Network (PAD-Net)~\cite{xu2018padnet} to exchange context among appliance-specific feature streams. Scaled dot-product attention~\cite{vaswani2017attention} is evaluated across appliances at each time $t$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_t=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}_t\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt{d}}
\right),\qquad
\mathbf{M}_t=\mathbf{A}_t\mathbf{V}_t.
\label{eq:relation-attention}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{Q}_t$, $\mathbf{K}_t$, and $\mathbf{V}_t$ are learned projections of the stacked appliance representations, and $d$ is their projection width. The attended context is projected back to the feature dimension and added to each private stream through a gated residual connection.

\textbf{Power-State Output Heads:}
For each appliance, two time-shared FC heads predict raw standardized power $\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)$ and a state logit $a_i(t)$. Following the state-gating principle of SGN~\cite{shin2019sgn}, the ON-state probability $\hat s_i(t)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_i(t))$ softly gates the power prediction:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_i(t)=\hat s_i(t)\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)
+[1-\hat s_i(t)]y_i^{(0)},
\label{eq:soft-power-gate}
\end{equation}
where $y_i^{(0)}=-\mu_i/\sigma_i$ is the standardized zero-power anchor. Hence, a low ON-state probability smoothly moves the estimate towards zero power. Predictions are converted back to watts using the appliance training mean $\mu_i$ and standard deviation $\sigma_i$, and negative values are clipped to zero. The gate couples the state and power predictions while retaining a separate waveform regressor.

\subsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss}

The model is trained jointly over all appliances, as in multi-task NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm,sun2025transfer}. Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}$ denote the power and state losses for appliance $i$, respectively. The joint objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}
+\lambda r_i\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}
\right),
\qquad \lambda=0.8.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here $r_i$ is an appliance-wise balancing factor defined below.

\textit{Power loss:}
The power objective combines waveform fidelity and switching fidelity:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}=
\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wave},i}}_{\text{power-level fidelity}}
+\underbrace{0.15\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trans},i}}_{\text{transition fidelity}}.
\label{eq:power-loss}
\end{equation}
The waveform and transition terms both use mean squared error (MSE), applied to power values and temporal differences, respectively. The expressions below are written for one window; in implementation, both terms are averaged over the batch and time dimensions. Let $y_i(t)$ and $\hat y_i(t)$ denote standardized target and predicted power, and let $z_i(t)\in\{0,1\}$ denote the ON-OFF label. For a temporal index set $\mathcal{S}$, define
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathcal{S}}(u,v)=
\frac{1}{\max(|\mathcal{S}|,1)}
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(u(t)-v(t)\bigr)^2.
\label{eq:temporal-mse}
\end{equation}
The waveform term evaluates power errors over all samples $\mathcal{T}_0=\{1,\ldots,T\}$, ON-state samples $\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{on}}_i=\{t\in\mathcal{T}_0:z_i(t)=1\}$, and OFF-state samples $\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{off}}_i=\{t\in\mathcal{T}_0:z_i(t)=0\}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wave},i}={}&
\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathcal{T}_0}(y_i,\hat y_i)
+\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{on}}_i}(y_i,\hat y_i)\\
&+0.5\,\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{off}}_i}(y_i,\hat y_i).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:waveform-loss}
\end{equation}
The first term measures global waveform fidelity; the ON-state term prevents sparse activations from being dominated by OFF samples, and the lower-weight OFF-state term constrains inactive predictions.

To additionally constrain changes in power, let $\Delta y_i(t)=y_i(t)-y_i(t-1)$ and define $\Delta\hat y_i(t)$ analogously. The transition term compares these differences whenever the appliance is ON at either adjacent sample, over $\mathcal{T}^{\Delta}_i=\{t\in\{2,\ldots,T\}:z_i(t)=1\ \text{or}\ z_i(t-1)=1\}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trans},i}=
\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathcal{T}^{\Delta}_i}(\Delta y_i,\Delta\hat y_i).
\label{eq:transition-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{State loss:}
The state objective combines weighted binary cross-entropy (BCE) with explicit false-positive control:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}=
\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE},i}}_{\text{class-balanced state detection}}
+\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}}_{\text{false-positive control}}.
\label{eq:state-loss}
\end{equation}
For a mini-batch of $B$ windows, let $\hat s_{b,t,i}\in(0,1)$ be the predicted ON-state probability and $z_{b,t,i}\in\{0,1\}$ the target label. The class-balanced BCE term uses $w_i=\min\{(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i,12\}$, determined by the training ON-state proportion $\pi_i$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE},i}=-\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}\bigl[&
w_i z_{b,t,i}\log\hat s_{b,t,i}\\
&+(1-z_{b,t,i})\log(1-\hat s_{b,t,i})\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weighted-bce}
\end{equation}
The false-positive term penalizes confident ON-state predictions on OFF samples:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}=
\frac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})\hat s_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max\left(\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i}),1\right)}.
\label{eq:false-positive-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{Bounded appliance-wise balancing:}
To account for the different numerical scales of regression and classification losses, a bounded loss-ratio weighting is used. It is motivated by loss-ratio balancing in SAMNet~\cite{liu2022samnet} and differs from gradient-norm balancing in GradNorm~\cite{chen2018gradnorm}. The global reference ratio $g$ bounds the appliance-specific ratio $r_i$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g&=
\frac{\sum_j\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},j}}
{\sum_j\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},j}},\\[-1pt]
r_i&=\min\!\left\{3g,\max\!\left\{
\frac{g}{3},
\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}}
{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}}
\right\}\right\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:balanced-ratio}
\end{equation}
At each mini-batch update, $g$ and $r_i$ are evaluated from the current losses and held fixed during back-propagation. The three-fold bounds limit each local ratio to $[g/3,3g]$, preventing extreme weights from dominating optimization.

\subsection{Focal-Event Background-Stratified Mixing}
\label{sec:training-strategy}

Training uses recorded and recomposed aggregate windows with equal probability. Recorded windows preserve the observed joint demand. Figure~\ref{fig:focal-mix} summarizes the sampling and construction of the recomposed windows. These windows extend synthetic aggregate construction in Neural NILM~\cite{kelly2015neural} by pairing appliance events with recorded background demand from other training windows~\cite{liu2026recomposition}, reducing reliance on house-specific event-background correlations. For a recorded sequence, the background demand is estimated by the nonnegative residual
\begin{equation}
R_t=\left[x_t-\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{t,i}\right]_+.
\label{eq:training-residual}
\end{equation}
Here $[u]_+=\max(u,0)$. Unlike $b_t$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:aggregate}, $R_t$ can also include measurement and timing discrepancies. For each recomposed window, the residual and appliance traces are drawn from independently selected training windows:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_t=R_t^{(k_0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\qquad
\tilde{P}_{t,i}=P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},\quad
\tilde{z}_{t,i}=z_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\label{eq:training-recomposition}
\end{equation}
where $k_0,k_1,\ldots,k_N$ denote independently sampled window indices. In Step~1 of Fig.~\ref{fig:focal-mix}, one focal appliance $i^\star$ is selected uniformly, and its trace is drawn from a window containing at least one ON sample; the remaining appliance traces are sampled independently. In Step~2, a non-empty bin of median residual power is selected uniformly from $[0,100)$, $[100,200)$, $[200,400)$, $[400,800)$, and $[800,\infty)$~W, and a background window is sampled uniformly within that bin. The selected traces and background are then added according to Equation~\eqref{eq:training-recomposition}, as illustrated in the lower panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:focal-mix}. The power and state labels of every selected appliance trace are retained. Recomposition is used only during training; validation and test use recorded aggregates and aligned labels.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}
Two public residential datasets are used: the UK Domestic Appliance-Level Electricity (UK-DALE) dataset~\cite{kelly2015ukdale} and the REFIT electrical-load dataset~\cite{murray2017refit}. The target appliances are the kettle, fridge, dishwasher, washer, and microwave, and all signals are resampled at 8~s.

The experiment uses a whole-house split: UK-DALE house 1 and REFIT houses 3, 5, 9, and 11 for training; UK-DALE house 5 and REFIT house 2 for validation; and UK-DALE house 2 and REFIT house 20 for testing. The resulting training-validation-test ratio is $5{:}2{:}2$ by house count. Because each house contributes one continuous 5-week block, this also corresponds to 55.6\%, 22.2\%, and 22.2\% of the nominal recording duration, respectively. The held-out blocks are centred within the previously established eight-week recording intervals and are selected without reference to appliance events or model performance. Normalization statistics are estimated from the training houses only.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocess}

After 8~s resampling, no additional temporal alignment is applied; residual timing discrepancies remain a measurement limitation. Rows missing any required aggregate, appliance-power, or state-label value are excluded, and windows do not cross timestamp discontinuities.

Let $n$ index windows and $t$ index samples within a window. The aggregate mean and standard deviation, $\mu_x$ and $\sigma_x$, and the corresponding appliance-$i$ statistics, $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$, are estimated from the training houses. The standardized input power, $\bar{x}_{n,t}$, and target power, $y_{n,t,i}$, are defined as
\begin{equation}
\bar x_{n,t}=\frac{x_{n,t}-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\qquad
y_{n,t,i}=\frac{P_{n,t,i}-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}.
\label{eq:power-normalization}
\end{equation}

State labels are precomputed before windowing. For appliances marked in Table~\ref{tab:label}, isolated spikes are first removed on a label-only copy of the resampled power trace. Each sample is then labeled ON when this trace reaches its appliance-specific threshold $\tau_i^{P}$. OFF gaps of at most $G_i$ are merged; subsequently, complete ON runs shorter than $D_i$ are removed. Runs touching a sequence boundary are retained because their full duration is unobserved. Thus, $\tau_i^{P}$, $G_i$, and $D_i$ denote the ON-power threshold, maximum OFF-gap duration, and minimum ON duration for appliance $i$, respectively. Table~\ref{tab:label} specifies their physical-time values, which are converted to 8~s samples by half-up rounding before labeling. UK-DALE house~5 uses a 30~s fridge gap.

\label{sec:design}

At 8~s per sample, each 1024-sample input-output window spans 136.5~min of household measurements.

For MART-NILM, checkpoint selection uses only the validation houses. Mean absolute error (MAE) in standardized power coordinates and average precision (AP) are computed from the validation outputs, and the retained checkpoint minimizes
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{val}}=\mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{val}}
+1-\mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{macro}}_{\mathrm{val}},
\label{eq:checkpoint-score}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{val}}$ is the macro mean
absolute error over appliances in standardized power coordinates, and the AP
term summarizes the precision-recall curve without selecting a decision
threshold. For each model and appliance $i$, let
$\hat s_{t,i}\in[0,1]$ denote the predicted ON probability at sample $t$.
After checkpoint selection, an appliance-specific threshold
$\gamma_i\in\{0.05,0.06,\ldots,0.98\}$ is selected on the validation houses to
maximize appliance-level F1 after fixed temporal post-processing is
applied. At test time, the decoded state label, $\hat{z}_{t,i}$, is obtained as
\begin{equation}
\hat z_{t,i}=\mathbb{I}[\hat s_{t,i}\geq\gamma_i],
\label{eq:state-decoding}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function. For the microwave, fixed
temporal post-processing removes predicted ON runs shorter than 32~s and merges OFF gaps
of at most 24~s. The decoded state is also applied to the power output,
$\hat P^{\mathrm{eval}}_{t,i}=\hat z_{t,i}\hat P_{t,i}$. Thresholds and
temporal rules are frozen before testing and applied unchanged to both test
houses; test labels are not used to select $\gamma_i$.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Each appliance is evaluated on power and on the ON-OFF state. For an evaluated record of length $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$, $P_t$ is the recorded power in watts and $\hat P_t^{\mathrm{eval}}$ is the state-decoded prediction. The label $z_t$ equals 1 when the appliance is recorded ON and 0 when it is OFF. $\hat z_t$ is the predicted label.

Results are reported separately for the held-out UK-DALE house~2 and REFIT house~20. The disjoint household protocol evaluates cross-house generalization without using any training or validation house for testing. Within each test house, the Avg. column is the macro average over the five appliances; architecture-ablation summaries give the two houses equal weight. All reported results are mean $\pm$ standard deviation over three random seeds, which quantifies optimization variability on the fixed cross-house split. The evaluation considers complementary power-estimation and state-detection metrics.

\subsubsection{Power}
Power error is reported using MAE, ON-state MAE (ON MAE), and blockwise signal aggregate error (SAE). MAE uses every sample; ON MAE is the same quantity evaluated only at samples with $z_t=1$, so that reconstruction quality during appliance operation remains visible:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{rec}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{rec}}}\left|P_t-\hat P_t^{\mathrm{eval}}\right|.
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
Following MATNilm~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}, SAE measures the mean absolute error of average power over non-overlapping 160~min blocks ($L=1200$ samples):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SAE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=(k-1)L+1}^{kL}\left(P_t-\hat P_t^{\mathrm{eval}}\right)\right|,
\qquad K=\left\lfloor\frac{T_{\mathrm{rec}}}{L}\right\rfloor.
\label{eq:sae}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{On-Off State}
The recorded label $z_t$ follows the rules in Table~\ref{tab:label}, and $\hat{z}_t$ is the predicted binary state. For each appliance, $\mathrm{TP}$ (true positives) counts samples correctly identified as ON ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=1$), $\mathrm{FP}$ (false positives) counts inactive samples falsely detected as ON ($z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=1$), and $\mathrm{FN}$ (false negatives) counts active samples missed as OFF ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=0$):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
Precision is the fraction of predicted ON samples that are truly ON, and recall is the fraction of actual ON samples detected. F1 is their harmonic mean; true negatives ($\mathrm{TN}$, $z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=0$) are excluded to prevent long idle background periods from artificially inflating the score.
To evaluate state ranking independently of the calibrated decision threshold, we also report AP from the continuous state probability. Let $\mathcal{P}_n$ and $\mathcal{R}_n$ denote precision and recall after the $n$th distinct probability threshold, with $\mathcal{R}_0=0$. The non-interpolated AP is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP}=\sum_n\left(\mathcal{R}_n-\mathcal{R}_{n-1}\right)\mathcal{P}_n.
\label{eq:average-precision}
\end{equation}
Unlike F1, AP does not use the appliance-specific threshold or the temporal state post-processing fitted on the validation houses.

The components make complementary contributions rather than improving every metric uniformly. \subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Two limitations remain. First, microwave detection is a recurrent NILM difficulty rather than one specific to MART-NILM. Earlier neural and multi-appliance studies report weaker or highly variable microwave performance~\cite{kelly2015neural,faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm}, while SAMNet shows that similar-power background loads can be mistaken for the target appliance~\cite{liu2022samnet}. Its short, intermittent, and variable-power activations compound this ambiguity. Focal-event mixing mitigates the problem across several background-demand conditions in Fig.~\ref{fig:focal-background}, but does not remove it. Second, performance remains sensitive to the background-demand level: under high aggregate demand, overlapping non-target activity can obscure appliance transitions even when the target signature is unchanged. Domain variation in NILM should therefore encompass both appliance-signature shifts and changes in household background-demand composition. Future work should model these two sources jointly across houses and regions rather than treating appliance waveform variation alone.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposed MART-NILM and two complementary training strategies for joint appliance power and state estimation from low-frequency aggregate measurements. The architecture extracts shared temporal evidence, separates appliance-specific representations, and exchanges relational context before state-gated power prediction. To support learning from sparse activations, focal-event background-stratified mixing varies the background demand surrounding appliance events, while bounded appliance-wise balancing coordinates their power and state objectives. Across three seeds, MART-NILM achieves appliance-average MAEs of $7.3$ and $10.9$~W and macro-F1 scores of $0.88$ and $0.72$ on the UK-DALE and REFIT test houses, respectively, outperforming three reproduced baselines on both measures with $1.373$ million parameters. The ablations support the complementary roles of temporal modeling, appliance specialization, and the training strategies. Together, these findings support compact joint power-state estimation as a smart-meter observation layer for residential demand-side monitoring.
