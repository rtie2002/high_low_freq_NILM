\begin{abstract}
Appliance-level load visibility supports residential demand-side management, but aggregate meters do not directly reveal individual consumption or operating states. Non-intrusive load monitoring estimates these quantities without dedicated appliance sensors. Multi-appliance disaggregation shares computation across loads, yet sparse activations under limited background conditions and unequal power--state loss scales complicate joint learning. This paper proposes a compact multi-appliance relational temporal network. A shared temporal convolutional encoder, appliance-specific decoders, and relational attention jointly estimate five appliance powers and states. Focal-event background-stratified mixing exposes active appliance traces to varied residual loads, while bounded appliance-wise balancing adjusts each classification objective relative to its corresponding regression loss. Both strategies operate only during training. Experiments on two public residential datasets use disjoint training, validation, and test houses and preserve recorded aggregates. Across three seeds, the proposed model achieves appliance-average mean absolute errors of 7.5 and 10.8 watts and macro-averaged F1 scores of 0.88 and 0.72 on the respective test houses, outperforming three reproduced baselines on both measures. With 1.373 million parameters and lower training time under the evaluated configurations, the method offers a favorable estimation--cost trade-off for residential load monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Residential energy management requires information about both the magnitude and operating state of individual loads. Aggregate metering provides household demand, but not this appliance-level visibility. Dedicated submetering can supply detailed feedback~\cite{delmas2013information}, at the cost of installing and maintaining additional sensors. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) estimates appliance consumption from aggregate measurements and offers a less instrument-intensive basis for residential load analysis~\cite{zoha2012nonintrusive,gopinath2020energy}. To support residential load observability, a NILM estimator must identify appliance activity as well as reconstruct appliance power: low average power error can conceal missed operating periods, particularly for appliances that remain off for most of the record.

Classical NILM identifies appliance signatures and infers appliance states with finite-state or factorial hidden Markov models~\cite{hart1992nilm,kolter2012fhmm}. Deep approaches instead learn representations directly from aggregate windows~\cite{kelly2015neural}, reducing the need to specify signatures and state-transition models manually. Regardless of the estimator, a recorded aggregate contains demand outside the monitored target set. For $M$ target appliances, the measured power is
\begin{equation}
x_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M} P_{t,i} + b_t + \epsilon_t,
\label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $P_{t,i}$ is target power, $b_t$ is unmonitored household demand, and $\epsilon_t$ represents measurement and timing discrepancies. This residual background can exceed a low-power target and varies between households, so a familiar appliance event can occur in an unfamiliar aggregate context. House-held-out evaluation on recorded aggregates is therefore needed; results from different sensing and partitioning protocols are not directly interchangeable~\cite{dasilva2026nilm}.

Within this setting, two common deep NILM modelling paradigms are single-appliance and multi-appliance estimation. Single-appliance networks predict one target per model and have advanced disaggregation accuracy~\cite{wang2022transformer,sun2023diffnilm,petralia2025nilmformer}, but repeating a model for every load repeats aggregate feature extraction and increases storage requirements. Recent deep NILM work has therefore increasingly studied multi-appliance estimation, in which one model jointly estimates a fixed set of $M$ target appliances from the same aggregate window~\cite{faustine2020unet,alami2023convnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget,liu2022samnet,sun2025transfer}. NILM research commonly considers two complementary tasks: power estimation and ON--OFF state detection. For appliance $i$ at time $t$, these tasks estimate a power trace $\hat y_{t,i}$ and an ON-state probability $\hat s_{t,i}$, respectively. Power estimation is a regression task, whereas state detection is multi-label classification in the multi-appliance setting because several appliances may be ON at the same time. For an aggregate window $\mathbf{x}$ of length $T$, the joint estimator is
\begin{equation}
\left(\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\hat{\mathbf{S}}\right)=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times M},\quad
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in[0,1]^{T\times M},
\label{eq:joint-estimation}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ collect the estimated power traces and ON-state probabilities, respectively.

Joint multi-appliance and multi-label estimation is more challenging than running $M$ independent predictors. Concurrent activations provide useful cross-appliance context, but their superposed transitions can mask a weak target beneath other loads or the residual background~\cite{faustine2020unet,alami2023convnilm,xiong2024matnilm}. A joint architecture must exchange cross-appliance information while retaining appliance-specific evidence and coherent power--state outputs. It should also avoid repeating costly aggregate feature extraction across appliances.

Training raises two further difficulties. First, sparse events in a finite record occur under only a subset of residual-background conditions. A model can then use residual-background correlations as a shortcut instead of the appliance signature; this can fail when those correlations change across households~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. Second, appliance power ranges and on-state prevalences differ substantially. Existing joint objectives use a shared regression--classification balance, either as a fixed coefficient or a global loss-ratio weight~\cite{sun2025transfer,liu2022samnet}. Such a shared balance cannot represent appliance-specific scales; when derived from joint losses, a large power error for one appliance also changes the relative state weight of the others. These observations motivate control over both residual-background conditions and the power--state balance of each appliance.

To address these issues, we propose the Multi-Appliance Relational Temporal Network for NILM (MART-NILM), a relational temporal model for joint power and ON--OFF state estimation under residual-background variation.

The main contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A compact multi-appliance architecture, MART-NILM, is designed for
    joint power and state estimation. It extracts a shared temporal
    representation once and combines appliance-specific decoding with
    cross-appliance relational context to produce five paired power--state
    outputs in a single forward pass.

    \item Two training-only strategies address sparse events,
    residual-background variation, and appliance-wise power--state imbalance.
    Focal-event background-stratified mixing exposes activations to a controlled
    range of recorded residual-background conditions, while bounded
    appliance-wise balancing independently aligns the power and state
    objectives of each appliance. Neither strategy adds inference-time
    computation.

    \item A house-held-out evaluation uses recorded aggregates from UK-DALE
    and REFIT. Three-seed
    baseline comparisons, architecture and training ablations, and
    background-conditioned analyses quantify power estimation, state
    detection, computational cost, and remaining failure modes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

\subsection{Multi-Appliance Energy Disaggregation}
\label{sec:rel-multi-appliance}

Early deep-learning NILM predominantly deployed single-appliance models~\cite{kelly2015neural,zhang2018sequence,shin2019sgn,yue2020bert4nilm,petralia2025nilmformer}. While isolating a single target simplifies network optimization, monitoring $M$ household loads necessitates training and storing $M$ independent models, multiplies aggregate feature-extraction passes, and ignores simultaneous appliance interactions~\cite{alami2023convnilm,xiong2024matnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget}.

Multi-appliance disaggregation overcomes these limitations by estimating multiple target loads simultaneously from a single aggregate window~\cite{faustine2020unet,liu2022samnet,li2023onemany,sun2025transfer}. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:protocol}, architectural strategies have evolved from shared convolutional stems with independent output heads (e.g., Conv-NILM-Net~\cite{alami2023convnilm} and MATNilm~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}) to multi-branch representations and mixture-of-experts designs~\cite{liu2026recomposition}. However, existing multi-appliance networks largely decouple appliance decoding after the shared backbone, lacking explicit mechanisms for cross-stream relational context during concurrent switching events. Furthermore, evaluation setups across the literature vary widely: several studies evaluate on synthetic additive sums omitting unmonitored baseload, tune hyperparameters on subsets of the training buildings, or restrict testing to single-house configurations (Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). True residential scalability demands multi-appliance estimators capable of generalizing across unseen households with real, unmonitored residual demand.

\subsection{Multi-Label Appliance State Detection and Multi-Task Formulation}
\label{sec:rel-multilabel}

Because residential appliances switch and operate concurrently, appliance state identification is fundamentally a multi-label classification (MLC) problem rather than a single-label multiclass task: at any timestamp $t$, the operating state vector $\mathbf{z}_t \in \{0, 1\}^M$ contains multiple concurrent active entries. 

Early NILM literature approached simultaneous state detection by coupling handcrafted feature extraction (such as wavelets, statistical moments, and switching transients) with shallow MLC classifiers, including support vector machines (SVM) and decision trees~\cite{tabatabaei2017novel}. Although computationally lightweight, handcrafted descriptors are sensitive to noise and degrade markedly when evaluated across unseen dwellings with different voltage fluctuations and baseload profiles. Recent research has therefore embraced end-to-end deep MLC formulations. For instance, Tanoni et al.~\cite{tanoni2023weak} investigated convolutional recurrent networks trained on weakly labeled data, while Dash and Sahoo~\cite{dash2024jestie} developed an attention-based causal convolutional network for simultaneous multi-appliance state identification.

Simultaneous power regression and state detection within a multi-task learning framework offers mutual structural regularization~\cite{faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm,sun2025transfer}. Incorporating state prediction supplies hard or soft gating that suppresses phantom power predictions when appliances are idle~\cite{shin2019sgn}. However, multi-appliance multi-task training introduces an acute loss-scale discrepancy across appliances: high-power loads (e.g., washing machine heating elements) incur substantial mean-squared regression errors, whereas low-power or infrequent loads (e.g., fridges or microwaves) produce small numerical errors. Standard multi-task NILM formulations apply a single uniform power-to-state loss weighting across all appliances, allowing the regression gradients of dominant loads to overwhelm the state classification objectives of sparse appliances. This limitation motivates our bounded appliance-wise balancing scheme, which balances power and state objectives independently for each appliance.

These studies establish shared representations and cross-house evaluation as existing directions. Our focus is the interaction between residual-background coverage and appliance-wise power--state scaling in a compact joint estimator. The common evaluation holds out validation houses as well as test houses and retains recorded aggregates, allowing the effects of both training strategies to be examined alongside the architecture.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $x_t$ denote aggregate power and $P_{t,i}$ the power of target appliance $i$, as in \eqref{eq:aggregate}. In this work, $M=5$ and the power branch of \eqref{eq:joint-estimation} is represented on a standardized scale during training. The input contains no appliance measurements or hand-crafted channels. The unobserved remainder is
\begin{equation}
x_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}=b_{t}+\epsilon_{t}.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
Hence the network must distinguish appliance activity from both other targets and unmonitored household demand.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/martnilm_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture of MART-NILM. The aggregate input is processed by a multi-scale convolutional stem and a shared temporal encoder. Appliance-specific decoders form five feature streams, cross-appliance attention exchanges information among them, and separate power and state heads jointly produce gated power estimates and on--off probabilities.}
    \label{fig:martnilm-architecture}
\end{figure*}

MART-NILM follows the shared--private design in Fig.~\ref{fig:martnilm-architecture}. A shared encoder extracts evidence once from the aggregate; appliance-specific decoders then separate this evidence into five streams; finally, relation attention allows the streams to exchange information before joint power and state prediction. Temporal resolution is preserved throughout.

\textit{Shared aggregate encoder:}
The encoder combines a multi-branch convolutional neural network (CNN) stem~\cite{szegedy2015inception} with residual temporal convolution. Parallel kernels capture switching patterns at complementary local scales. Instance--batch normalization (IBN)~\cite{pan2018ibn} divides shallow channels equally between instance normalization (IN) and batch normalization (BN), combining per-window and batch-level normalization. This design is intended to reduce sensitivity to household-specific offsets while retaining complementary amplitude information. The resulting sequence is processed by five dilated temporal convolutional network (TCN) blocks~\cite{lea2017tcn}. Writing $\mathcal{T}_{\ell}$ for the 1D convolution, BN, rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and dropout in block $\ell$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)}
=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}+\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
\!\left(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)};d_{\ell}\right),
\qquad d_{\ell}\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}.
\label{eq:temporal-block}
\end{equation}
This produces the shared representation $\mathbf{H}$ without temporal downsampling, preserving the alignment between input transitions and appliance outputs.

\textit{Appliance decoding and relation modelling:}
Each decoder applies a channel gate~\cite{hu2018senet} and a local residual transformation to obtain an appliance-specific representation $\mathbf{F}_i$. These private streams prevent all appliances from being forced into the same representation. The relation module adapts the cross-task information-sharing principle of PAD-Net~\cite{xu2018padnet} to appliance-specific feature streams. To model simultaneous operation, scaled dot-product attention~\cite{vaswani2017attention} is evaluated across appliances at each time $t$, rather than across time:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_t=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}_t\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt{d}}
\right),\qquad
\mathbf{M}_t=\mathbf{A}_t\mathbf{V}_t.
\label{eq:relation-attention}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{Q}_t$, $\mathbf{K}_t$, and $\mathbf{V}_t$ are learned projections of the stacked appliance representations, and $d$ is their projection width. A gated residual connection adds the relation message $\mathbf{M}_t$ to each private stream, so cross-appliance context supplements rather than replaces appliance-specific evidence.

\textit{Joint power and state prediction:}
For each appliance, two time-shared fully connected (FC) heads produce a raw standardized power $\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)$ and a state logit $a_i(t)$. The on-state probability $\hat s_i(t)=\sigma(a_i(t))$ is both the state prediction and a differentiable gate on the power branch~\cite{shin2019sgn,xiong2024matnilm}:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_i(t)=\hat s_i(t)\hat y_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(t)
+[1-\hat s_i(t)]y_i^{\mathrm{off}},
\label{eq:soft-power-gate}
\end{equation}
where $y_i^{\mathrm{off}}=-\mu_i/\sigma_i$ is zero watts on the standardized scale. Thus an on probability near one preserves the regressed waveform, whereas a probability near zero moves the estimate towards zero watts. The final prediction in physical units is
\begin{equation}
\hat P_i(t)=\left[\sigma_i\hat y_i(t)+\mu_i\right]_+,
\qquad [u]_+=\max(u,0).
\label{eq:power-in-watts}
\end{equation}
The state head therefore provides an explicit operating-state prior while the power head retains the appliance waveform.

\subsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss}

The model is trained jointly over all appliances, as in multi-task NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet,xiong2024matnilm,virtsionis2023multitarget}. Let $b=1,\ldots,B$ index windows in a mini-batch, $t=1,\ldots,T$ index samples, and $i=1,\ldots,M$ index appliances. The standardized target and predicted powers are $y_{b,t,i}$ and $\hat y_{b,t,i}$; $z_{b,t,i}\in\{0,1\}$ is the on--off label; and $\hat s_{b,t,i}\in(0,1)$ is the predicted on probability. The objective separates power regression from state classification:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power}}}
+\lambda\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{M}r_i\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state}}},
\qquad \lambda=0.8.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Hence every appliance contributes one power loss and one state loss, and the five appliance losses are summed to train a single network.

\textit{Power loss:}
For appliance $i$, let $e_{b,t,i}=\hat y_{b,t,i}-y_{b,t,i}$, $N_i^{\mathrm{on}}=\sum_{b,t}z_{b,t,i}$, and $N_i^{\mathrm{off}}=BT-N_i^{\mathrm{on}}$. The overall, on-state, and off-state mean squared errors are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{MSE}_i&=\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}e_{b,t,i}^{2},\nonumber\\
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{on}}&=
\tfrac{\sum_{b,t}z_{b,t,i}e_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{on}},1)},\nonumber\\
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{off}}&=
\tfrac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})e_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{off}},1)}.
\label{eq:power-mse}
\end{align}
The conditional terms keep errors during the sparse on state visible instead of allowing the many off samples to dominate the regression objective.

To preserve switching behaviour, define $\Delta y_{b,t,i}=y_{b,t,i}-y_{b,t-1,i}$ and $m_{b,t,i}=\max(z_{b,t,i},z_{b,t-1,i})$. With $N_i^{\Delta}=\sum_{b,t=2}^{T}m_{b,t,i}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\Delta}=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{b,\,t=2}^{T}m_{b,t,i}
(\Delta\hat{y}_{b,t,i}-\Delta y_{b,t,i})^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\Delta},1)}.
\label{eq:transition-loss}
\end{equation}
Window-level energy is measured in watts using $P_{b,t,i}=[\sigma_i y_{b,t,i}+\mu_i]_+$ and $\hat P_{b,t,i}=[\sigma_i\hat y_{b,t,i}+\mu_i]_+$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RE}_i=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{\bigl|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\hat{P}_{b,t,i}-P_{b,t,i})\bigr|}
{\sum_{t=1}^{T}P_{b,t,i}+TP_0},
\quad P_0=10~\mathrm{W}.
\label{eq:energy-loss}
\end{equation}
The per-appliance power loss is therefore
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}={}&
\mathrm{MSE}_i+\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{on}}
+0.5\,\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\mathrm{off}}\\
&+0.15\,\mathrm{MSE}_i^{\Delta}+0.25\,\mathrm{RE}_i.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:power-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{State loss:}
Let $\pi_i$ denote the proportion of on samples for appliance $i$ in the training set. Its positive-class weight is $w_i=\min\{(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i,12\}$. The weighted binary cross-entropy is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{BCE}_i=-\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}\bigl[&
w_i z_{b,t,i}\log\hat s_{b,t,i}\\
&+(1-z_{b,t,i})\log(1-\hat s_{b,t,i})\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weighted-bce}
\end{equation}
An additional false-positive term penalizes high on probabilities when the appliance is off:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}=
\frac{\sum_{b,t}(1-z_{b,t,i})\hat s_{b,t,i}^{2}}
{\max(N_i^{\mathrm{off}},1)},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}=\mathrm{BCE}_i+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fp},i}.
\label{eq:state-loss}
\end{equation}

\textit{Appliance-wise task balance:}
Because MSE and BCE have different numerical scales, the state loss of each appliance is matched to its own power loss. Let
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r&=\operatorname{sg}\!\left(
\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power}}}
{\sum_j\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},j}+\varepsilon}\right),\\
q_i&=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{power},i}}
{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state},i}+\varepsilon},\qquad
r_i=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(q_i,\frac{r}{3},3r\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:balanced-ratio}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the weights and $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. Each appliance has its own power--state ratio, bounded relative to the joint ratio to limit extreme weights. The shared bounds retain some cross-appliance dependence; this is loss-scale balancing rather than direct gradient-norm equalization.

\subsection{Training Strategy}
\label{sec:training-strategy}

Training alternates between recorded and recomposed aggregate windows with equal probability. The recorded windows retain the natural joint distribution of household loads. The recomposed windows reduce dependence on appliance combinations and residual backgrounds observed in a particular house, following the use of synthetic aggregates in NILM~\cite{kelly2015neural,liu2026recomposition}. From each recorded training sequence, the non-target residual is
\begin{equation}
R_t=\left[x_t-\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}\right]_+.
\label{eq:training-residual}
\end{equation}
For a recomposed window, each appliance trace and the residual are drawn from independently selected training windows:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_t=R_t^{(k_0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\qquad
\tilde{P}_{t,i}=P_{t,i}^{(k_i)},\quad
\tilde{z}_{t,i}=z_{t,i}^{(k_i)},
\label{eq:training-recomposition}
\end{equation}
where $k_0,k_1,\ldots,k_M$ denote independent window indices. The additive construction therefore preserves the power and state labels of every selected appliance.

To prevent sparse events from disappearing from the recomposed set, one focal appliance $i^\star$ is selected uniformly for each synthetic window and its trace is drawn from windows containing at least one on sample. The residual window is selected by first sampling uniformly from the non-empty median-power intervals $[0,100)$, $[100,200)$, $[200,400)$, $[400,800)$, and $[800,\infty)$~W, and then sampling a window within that interval. The remaining appliance traces are sampled without this constraint. Recomposition is used only for training; validation and test retain the recorded aggregate and its aligned labels. Fig.~\ref{fig:focal-mix} summarises the two-step procedure.

The held-out blocks are centred within the previously established eight-week recording intervals and are selected without reference to appliance events or model performance. Normalization statistics are estimated from the training houses only.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocess}

Aggregate and appliance channels are independently mean-resampled onto an 8~s grid. The primary experiments retain the recorded timing; residual timing discrepancies remain a limitation of the measurements.

Let $\mu_x,\sigma_x$ and $\mu_i,\sigma_i$ be the aggregate and appliance statistics estimated from the training houses. The input and target powers are standardized as
\begin{equation}
\bar x_{n,t}=\frac{x_{n,t}-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\qquad
y_{n,t,i}=\frac{P_{n,t,i}-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}.
\label{eq:power-normalization}
\end{equation}

State labels are obtained before windowing from $z_{t,i}=\mathbb{I}[P_{t,i}\geq\tau_i]$, where $t=1,\ldots,N$ and $N$ is the length of the recorded sequence. Off gaps no longer than $G_i$ are joined to the surrounding on period, and on periods shorter than $D_i$ are discarded. Algorithm~\ref{alg:labeling} gives the full procedure; Table~\ref{tab:label} lists the per-appliance parameters. A 30~s fridge gap is used in UK-DALE house~5. Isolated spikes are removed only for the appliances marked in Table~\ref{tab:label}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{On/Off State Labeling for Appliance $i$}
\label{alg:labeling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} $P_{1:N}$ — resampled power ($N$ = recording length); $\tau$ — on-power threshold; $G$ — min-off gap (samples); $D$ — min-on duration (samples)
\STATE \textbf{Output:} binary label sequence $z_{1:N}$
\IF{spike removal enabled for appliance $i$}
    \STATE Remove isolated outliers from $P$ via rolling-window $z$-statistic
\ENDIF
\FOR{$t=1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
    \STATE $z_t \leftarrow \mathbb{I}[P_t \geq \tau]$ \COMMENT{threshold}
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each OFF run $[s,\,e)$ in $z$}
    \IF{$e - s \leq G$}
        \STATE $z_s,\ldots,z_{e-1} \leftarrow 1$ \COMMENT{close short gap}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each \emph{complete} ON run $[s,\,e)$ in $z$ (not at segment boundary)}
    \IF{$e - s < D$}
        \STATE $z_s,\ldots,z_{e-1} \leftarrow 0$ \COMMENT{remove brief event}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $z_{1:N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

MART-NILM is compared with UNet-NILM~\cite{faustine2020unet}, MAT-Conv~\cite{xiong2024matnilm}, and TransferNILM~\cite{sun2025transfer}. Each baseline retains the architecture, objective, and training hyperparameters of its source paper, while all models use the common house-held-out data protocol. Table~\ref{tab:cost} lists their settings. All four models are trained with Adam. UNet-NILM runs for the full 50 epochs. MAT-Conv stops after 30 epochs without improvement. TransferNILM jointly minimizes mean-squared power error and binary cross-entropy state error; it runs for at most 100 epochs and stops after 60 epochs without improvement in its published validation criterion. MART-NILM uses weight decay $10^{-4}$ and unit-norm gradient clipping. Its learning rate is reduced by a factor of 0.5 after 12 epochs without validation improvement, to a minimum of $10^{-5}$. Training is limited to 150 epochs; early stopping is disabled before epoch 100 and thereafter uses a patience of 55 epochs. A 1024-sample window covers about 136.5~min.

For MART-NILM, checkpoint selection and state calibration use only the validation houses. Average precision (AP) is computed from the continuous state probabilities, and the retained checkpoint minimizes
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{val}}=\mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{val}}
+1-\mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{macro}}_{\mathrm{val}},
\label{eq:checkpoint-score}
\end{equation}
where the AP term summarizes the precision--recall curve without selecting a
decision threshold. After checkpoint selection,
an appliance-specific threshold is chosen from $\{0.05,0.06,\ldots,0.98\}$ to
maximize validation F1. For the microwave, predicted on periods shorter than
32~s are removed and off gaps no longer than 24~s are joined. The resulting
binary state $\hat z_{t,i}$ is also applied to the power output,
$\hat P^{\mathrm{eval}}_{t,i}=\hat z_{t,i}\hat P_{t,i}$. The fitted thresholds
and temporal rules are then frozen and applied unchanged to both test houses.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Each appliance is evaluated on power and on the on--off state. For an evaluated record of length $T$, $P_{t}$ is the recorded power in watts and $\hat{P}_{t}$ is the predicted power. The label $z_{t}$ equals 1 when the appliance is recorded on and 0 when it is off. $\hat{z}_{t}$ is the predicted label. The Average column in Table~\ref{tab:results} is the mean of the five appliance scores. Here $T$ is the length of the record, not the training window of Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

For an appliance-level score $q_{h,i}$ of appliance $i$ in house $h$, we first form the appliance average within that house and then give every house equal weight,
\begin{equation}
\bar q_h=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{h,i},\qquad
\bar q_{\mathcal H}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal H|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\bar q_h.
\label{eq:house-macro}
\end{equation}
For F1, $\bar q_h$ is the macro-F1 over the five appliances. Checkpoint selection uses $\mathcal H=\{\text{UK-DALE~5},\text{REFIT~2}\}$, the two validation houses. UK-DALE~2 and REFIT~20 are excluded from checkpoint selection and reported separately as cross-house test cases. Their equal-house mean is used for the architecture-ablation summary. Because these test cases were also inspected during method development, they constitute a development-informed benchmark rather than an untouched confirmatory evaluation~\cite{cawley2010overfitting}. Three-seed variability measures training randomness on this fixed split, not variability across households.

\subsubsection{Power}
Power error is reported using MAE, ON MAE, and SAE. MAE uses every sample, whereas ON MAE is restricted to samples with $z_t=1$ so that reconstruction quality during appliance operation remains visible.
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|P_{t}-\hat{P}_{t}\right|.
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
SAE divides the record into consecutive blocks of $L=1200$ samples, or 160~min at 8~s. The index of a block is $k$, and $K=\lfloor T/L\rfloor$ is the number of complete blocks. A trailing block shorter than $L$ is omitted. SAE is the mean absolute error of the average power in each block,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SAE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=(k-1)L+1}^{kL}\left(P_{t}-\hat{P}_{t}\right)\right|.
\label{eq:sae}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{On-Off State}
The recorded label $z_t$ follows the rules in Table~\ref{tab:label}, and $\hat{z}_t$ is the predicted binary state. For each appliance, $\mathrm{TP}$ (true positives) counts samples correctly identified as on ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=1$), $\mathrm{FP}$ (false positives) counts inactive samples falsely detected as on ($z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=1$), and $\mathrm{FN}$ (false negatives) counts active samples missed as off ($z_t=1, \hat{z}_t=0$):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
Precision is the fraction of predicted on samples that are truly on, and recall is the fraction of actual on samples detected. F1 is their harmonic mean; true negatives ($\mathrm{TN}$, $z_t=0, \hat{z}_t=0$) are excluded to prevent long idle background periods from artificially inflating the score.
To evaluate state ranking independently of the calibrated decision threshold, we also report AP from the continuous state probability. Let $\mathcal{P}_n$ and $\mathcal{R}_n$ denote precision and recall after the $n$th distinct probability threshold, with $\mathcal{R}_0=0$. The non-interpolated AP is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP}=\sum_n\left(\mathcal{R}_n-\mathcal{R}_{n-1}\right)\mathcal{P}_n.
\label{eq:average-precision}
\end{equation}
Unlike F1, AP does not use the appliance-specific threshold or the temporal state postprocessing fitted on the validation houses.

The multi-scale CNN is reduced to its central kernel while preserving its
width and residual path.  Removing the feature encoder retains only a
pointwise channel projection, and removing the temporal encoder uses an
identity mapping.  The appliance-decoder ablation sends the shared temporal
representation directly to five independent power/state readouts.  The final
variant retains these appliance-specific readouts but removes information
exchange between them.  These replacements preserve the joint prediction
task and isolate the contribution of each top-level module.

The shared temporal encoder gives the largest contribution: its removal
increases MAE by 1.44~W and ON MAE by 45.13~W, while macro-F1 falls from
0.796 to 0.710.  Removing the appliance-specific decoders increases ON MAE
by 22.90~W and reduces macro-F1 by 0.013.  The feature encoder provides a
smaller but consistent benefit to ON-state reconstruction and detection.
The multi-scale CNN has little effect on the aggregate averages, but its
benefit is concentrated on the sparse microwave task: on validation
UK-DALE~5 its removal increases microwave ON MAE from 589.49 to 696.45~W,
and on REFIT~20 microwave F1 falls from 0.549 to 0.512.  Relation attention
also exhibits a task trade-off.  Removing it lowers average ON MAE, but
reduces macro-F1 and lowers REFIT~20 microwave F1 from 0.549 to 0.496.
Waveform inspection agrees with the numerical results: removing temporal
encoding produces prolonged false fridge activations and poorly aligned
microwave plateaus, while removing the appliance decoders increases
fragmentation and amplitude error.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented MART-NILM for joint appliance power and state estimation from low-frequency aggregate measurements. Under the common five-week house-held-out benchmark, it achieves the lowest appliance-average power error and highest macro-F1 and average precision on both test houses among the reproduced models, using 1.373 million parameters. The shared temporal encoder and appliance-specific decoders provide the principal architectural gains. Focal-event mixing improves event coverage, while bounded appliance-wise balancing addresses unequal power--state loss scales; their combination improves validation detection without additional inference computation. The ablations also expose trade-offs, including limited microwave detection gains from the training strategies. These findings support compact joint estimation as a basis for residential load observability. Evidence remains restricted to two test houses inspected during development; broader household generalisation and downstream energy-management benefits require independent evaluation.
